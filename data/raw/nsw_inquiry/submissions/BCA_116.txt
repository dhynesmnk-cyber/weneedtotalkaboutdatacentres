Supporting everything we do

A normal day in modern Australia already involves constant interaction with data centres, even if most people
never notice it. Sending a message, transferring money, taking

For many people

3.2

A digitised world

The sector's scale means it can serve as a genuine partner in the state's renewable energy transition.

Data centrescan help anchor new renewable energy investment and support the expansion of modern grid transmission
infrastructure

-

25, with demand expected to rise to around six per cent by 2029

9

These figures highlight the importance of planning rather than alarm

4.1

A net reduction in energy burden

One of the most counterintuitive things about data centres is that they reduce

rather than increase the totalenergy consumed by computing. Data centres are an eiciency creating mechanism.
The alternative to modern data centres is a dispersed system in which computing takes place across thousands
of small, on
-
premise servers and devices located in homes and oice buildings. These systems typically run at
low utilisation and use less eicient hardware. As a result, they consume far more electricity overall. If all data
centres suddenly disappeared,

we would still need computers to do the same work, but we would
require seventimes more electricity

to do it
.
10

Clearly, data centres arethemost

eicient

way to manage our digital society

in a sustainable way
. Theyaggregate

computing workloads in purpose
-
built environments where operators can manage power usecarefully, maintain high equipment utilisation and deploy advanced cooling systems. Modern facilities use
sophisticated power management systems and highly eicient chips
specialised for dierent types of workloadsthat maximise the amount of computing delivered per unit of electricity.

4.2

Increasing eiciency

Data centres are only getting more eicient. Eiciency gains can be measured through Power Usage
Eectiveness, or PUE, the standard global metric for data centre eiciency. In 2007 the global average PUE was
around 2.5, meaning that for every unit of electricity used for computing, another 1.5 uni
ts were consumed bycooling and other support systems. Today the global average has fallen to around 1.58.
11

Australian facilitiesperform even better. Many report design PUE levels as low as 1.15, with median values around 1.30.
12

Specific hardware is also getting more eicient.
Modern AI chips have historically doubled their energy eiciency
roughly every two years.
13

This means that over time, many data centres will perform far more computing work
while using comparatively less electricity.

The rapid growth in data centre investment has produced a surge in applications for electricity grid connections.
Yet not every application represents a project that will be built. Many prospective

developers lodge connectionrequests early in the planning process to secure potential access to network capacity, even if the project itself
remains uncertain.

This dynamic has created what energy planners call phantom demand. In 2025 the Australian Energy Market
Operator(AEMO)
received around 44 gigawatts of connection requests linked to data centre proposals.
18

Onlyabout six gigawatts of project capacity is expected to proceed, with only
2.8 gigawatts of actual electricity drawonce those facilities reach maturity



Managing water

Water is among the most sensitive resource questions in a state that experiences periodic drought, faces long
-
term pressuresfrom

population growth, and is acutely aware of the need to manage natural resources carefully.

5.1

Proportionate potable water use
Current water use by data centres is small relative to overall supply. Similar to energy use, when
use of water isaggregatedinto data centres, it becomes far more eicient
.
Across Australia, data centres consume around 5.5gigalitres of water each year, which represents approximately 0.04 per cent of national potable water use. In
Sydney, data centres account for about 0.7 per cent of total water consumption.
21

Even with continued expansionof the sector, industry projections indicate that data centre water use is expected to remain below two per cent
of total potable water supply through to 2030.

5.2

Using recycled water

Data centre operators are investing in recycled water infrastructure, with projected industry investment estimated
to reach between $500 million and $1.1 billion by 2030.
22

Recycled water infrastructure benefits the broader community by reducing reliance on potable supplies and
improving the overall eiciency of the urban water cycle. Data centres, which can use non-
potable water forcooling systems, have a strong commercial

incentive to support these investments. For example, recycled water
now supplies 55 per cent of AirTrunks total use, and its Singapore site runs entirely on reclaimed water.
23

When recycled water networks are available, operators reduce operating costs while easing pressure on drinking
water supplies.

Government planning frameworks play an important role in enabling these investments. Utility partnerships, clear
planning approvals and infrastructure coordination make recycled water projects more viable.

Data centres haveshown a clear willingness to use recycled water and fund the infrastructure needed to connect to supply. But i
n
NSW
, access remains constrained. Policy settings should support broader availability of recycled water for
industrial users, including data centres, where demand and funding are already in place.

5.3

Better cooling technology
Cooling technology continues to evolve in ways that reduce water demand.
24

Modern data centres can operatecooling systems which significantly reduce or eliminate the need for continuous water supply.
Closed
-
loop liquid cooling

uses far less water than most people expect. The system circulates a fixed volume of
coolant through sealed pipes. That liquid absorbs heat from chips, then releases it through a heat exchanger. The
same fluid cycles again and again. It is not evaporated, and it is not discharged. Water only enters the system in
small amounts for initial filling or occasional top
-
ups. In steady operation, consumption is close to zero.

Air cooled systems can also reduce water consumption, but they do it in a dierent way. Many modern data
centres use dry cooling, where heat is rejected to ambient air through radiators or cooling coils. These systems
rely on fans and heat exchangers, not

evaporation. As a result, they use little or no water during normal operation.

Dry cooling works well in milder conditions, but its eiciency drops during hot summer periods, so systems
switch to evaporative cooling, which uses water but reduces electric
ity demand, reflecting a trade
-
o betweenwater use and energy eiciency.
