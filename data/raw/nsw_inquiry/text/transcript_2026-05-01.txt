The CHAIR:

Would you like to make a short opening statement?

BELINDA DENNETT:
Thank you, Chair and Committee, for the opportunity to appear today. My name
is Belinda Dennett
,

and I am the chief executive officer of Data Centres Australia, the peak body for the data
centre sector. Our members represent 86 per cent of operational data centre capacity across the country. I wish to
make a short opening statement that touches on five points. Firstly, data centres underpin a modern economy.
They run everything from banking, transport systems, air traffic control, emergency management systems, visa
processing, hospital systems, business applications and the like. They are critical inf
rastructure.

Secondly, the growth in data centre capacity is being driven by three things: the ongoing shift from
on
-
premise compute to the cloud, which brings with it a 67 per cent energy efficiency; the continued uptake of
new digital services; and the rapid adoptionby consumers and businesses of artificial intelligence. If Australia's
compute processing requirements to 2030 were met by hosting servers in the basements of buildings, or what we
call "on
-
premises" instead of data centres, electricity consumption would
be 22 terawatt hours more. Data centresare an efficient way to run a digital economy.

Thirdly, the situation in Australia is very different to the US. The scale is significantly smaller, and the
regulatory environment is vastly different. Data centre operators pay their way. The national electricity rules
require all large load users, including data centres, to pay 100 per cent connection costs and augmentations to the
network. Data centre operators will pay $10.3 billion in energy infrastructure to 2030, and $1.1 billion of that is
excess capacity available for public use. Data centre operators do not receive subsidies or tax incentives. Fourth,
there is significant misinformation around water use in the public debate. The ABS water accounts show that data
centre water use is 0.04 per cent of Australia's water use, and less than 1 per cent o
f Sydney's water use. The25

per cent of Sydney's water by 2035 that has been widely repeated in the media and in submissions to this
inquiry is not based in fact. It was dismissed by IPART
the independent pricing regulator
as beingunverifiable, and it should be dismissed.

My fifth point is that the opportunity to attract AI infrastructure investment to Australia is not just an
opportunity for foreign investment. The countries that build the infrastructure that supports the greatest
technological shift we have seen will capture the jobs, the economic value, national competitiveness and the ability
to influence how AI reflects their own values, culture and laws. Those that don't will become a consumer of
someone else's technology under someone else's rules.

Data centres are not a problem to be managed. They areessential infrastructure, generating the highest gross value added per unit of energy of any industrial sector. We
welcome this inquiry and I'm happy to take your questions.
