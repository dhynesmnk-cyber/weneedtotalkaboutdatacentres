ST CLAIR , New South Wales
Message
I object

1. Proximity to residential areas
The proposed facility is too close to residential properties for a development of this scale and intensity.
The Department should require detailed assessment of the impact on nearby homes.
Particular consideration should be given to 24-hour operational noise, including low-frequency and tonal noise, and the potential for sleep disturbance.

2. Proximity to schools
The proposal is also located in close proximity to two primary schools and two high schools.
The potential impact on children, teachers and school communities requires specific assessment, including:
construction noise, dust and vibration;
diesel emissions;
heavy vehicle movements;
generator testing;
operational noise;
traffic and pedestrian safety; and
emergency and fire risks associated with large-scale diesel storage.
A specific assessment of the impact on each nearby school should be undertaken before the application is determined.

3. Water supply and pressure
I am concerned about the amount of water required to operate a facility containing 936 cooling units and the potential impact on local water infrastructure.
The applicant should demonstrate that the development will not reduce the water pressure, reliability or availability of water to surrounding residential properties, particularly during periods of peak demand, drought or emergency conditions.
The Department should require independent assessment of:
peak and annual water demand;
the capacity of existing water infrastructure;
potential impacts on residential water pressure;
emergency water requirements; and
the source and disposal of water used by the facility.
Residential water supply should not be compromised to service a private industrial development.

4. Water quality and chemical contamination
The cooling systems and associated infrastructure may require treatment chemicals and other substances.
The applicant should identify all chemicals to be stored, used or discharged and demonstrate that they cannot contaminate groundwater, stormwater or water infrastructure.
Independent baseline and ongoing testing should be required to detect any contamination, chemical accumulation or deterioration in local water quality.
This is particularly important given the proximity of residential properties and schools.

5. Noise and diesel generators
The proposed 852 diesel generators and 936 cooling units create significant concerns about ongoing noise and air emissions.
Generator testing and emergency operation should be assessed separately from normal operational noise, with particular attention to night-time impacts.
Strict, independently monitored noise limits should apply at the nearest residential properties and schools.

6. Diesel storage, fire and environmental risk
The proposed storage of approximately 14,430 kL of diesel represents a substantial fire, spill and contamination risk.
The Department should require independent assessment of the consequences of fire, fuel leaks, spills and contamination of soil, groundwater and stormwater, including the potential impact on nearby residential properties and schools.

7. Traffic and construction
Construction of a facility of this magnitude will generate significant heavy vehicle movements, noise, dust and congestion.
The traffic assessment should specifically address impacts on residential streets, school zones, pedestrians and nearby intersections.

8. Cumulative impacts
This development should not be assessed in isolation.
The Mamre Road and Kemps Creek precinct is experiencing significant industrial development. The Department should assess the cumulative impact of this project together with other existing and proposed developments, particularly regarding noise, traffic, water demand, air quality, electricity infrastructure, diesel storage and loss of residential amenity.

9. Requested outcome
Given the scale of the proposed development and its proximity to residential properties and four schools, I request that the application be refused unless the applicant can demonstrate through independent evidence that the development will not materially adversely affect residential amenity, school environments, water supply, water quality, air quality, safety or local infrastructure.
If approval is contemplated, strict and enforceable conditions should require independent monitoring of noise, air quality, water quality, water consumption, water pressure, generator operation, fuel storage and construction impacts, with monitoring results made publicly available

ST PETERS , New South Wales
Message
I am writing on behalf of the Sydney Knitting Nannas & Friends, a group of older people who want to ensure a healthy climate and environment for generations to come. Our membership includes a range of retired and working professionals in fields such as public health research, others with legal qualifications and environmental citizen science experience in the areas of waste, water and biodiversity.

We object to the Construction and operation of a data centre campus in western Sydney.

1. Environmental Impacts

Electricity Demand and Emissions

The CEFC/Baringa report estimates Australian data centre electricity consumption could rise to between 24-35TWh annually by 2035, approximately 8–11% of Australian electricity consumption (up from 1-2% today) (CEFC & Baringa, 2025). The report warns that unmanaged growth could increase wholesale electricity prices by 26% in NSW, while increasing National Electricity Market emissions by 14% (CEFC & Baringa, 2025). Energy-intensive AI infrastructure creates demand that is largely continuous, unlike many industrial loads that can be reduced during peak demand periods.

This centre will emit a current maximum of 1,700,709t CO₂-e/year. This is equivalent to the total annual carbon footprint of between 85,035 and 113,381 average Australian homes.

Recommendation 1: Aiming for Net Zero isn’t enough - it needs to be a requirement that the campus will be running with 100% renewables immediately.

Water Consumption

Water consumption is an increasingly significant concern, particularly in Western Sydney. Clear priority should be given to human consumption, and planning must consider the possibility of extended future droughts.
Recommendation 2: Mandate annual public reporting of water consumption, water sources and recycling rates. Make a requirement (not a “nice to have”) that this centre uses non-drinkable water sources.

Land Use and Ecological Impacts

The concentration of development in western Sydney raises broader concerns regarding land availability, biodiversity loss, urban heat effects and competing demands for housing. The NSW Government has already restricted a new data centre development in Macquarie Park because of competition with housing needs (CEFC & Baringa, 2025).

Australia should avoid repeating mistakes seen internationally, where planning systems failed to account for cumulative impacts until
development had already accelerated.

Recommendation 3: The ESD report says “The development in its current design is therefore likely to contribute to localised nighttime warming whilst not exacerbating daytime peak temperatures, though localised hotspots may still occur on specific surfaces, particularly those with low reflectivity or limited shading. These results provide a quantitative basis for considering mitigation strategies such as increased vegetation, highly reflectivity roofs, and shaded areas, which can help reduce heat retention and improve local thermal comfort during night-time periods.”

2. Social and Community Impacts

Property Values and Community Amenity

Recent Australian polling found that 83% of Australians would require a discount to purchase a house near a data centre, with the average discount exceeding $35,000 because of environmental issues (Realestate.com.au, 2026). While this evidence is survey-based rather than transaction-based, it indicates a significant emerging social licence issue.

Employment Claims Should Be Scrutinised

Governments and developers frequently promote job creation benefits. However, data centres create most employment during construction. Once operational, they employ relatively small workforces compared with manufacturing, healthcare, education or other industries occupying equivalent land. Policy should therefore distinguish between temporary construction jobs and long-term employment.

3. Economic Return on Investment for Australia

A key question is whether Australia receives sufficient economic value from hosting data centres. Proponents cite estimates that AI and automation could contribute up to $600 billion to GDP by 2030 (CEFC & Baringa, 2025).

However, GDP growth should not be accepted as proof of public benefit. Important questions remain:

Do benefits justify the environmental and other costs?
How much revenue remains in Australia, and how much profit is repatriated overseas?
How many permanent Australian jobs are created?
How much taxpayer-funded infrastructure supports these developments?

Please reconsider this proposal, there does not need to be a data centre in Penrith.

Yours sincerely,
Wendy Dorrington
For the Sydney Knitting Nannas & Friends
25 August 2026

GLENMORE PARK , New South Wales
Message
I am writing to object to State Significant Development Application SSD-92743706 for the Mamre Road Data Centre Campus.

I ask that the Department, and ultimately the Independent Planning Commission, to refuse this project. My objection is grounded in five main areas of concern: water resources, air quality, noise, energy consumption, and broader environmental and cumulative impacts. I outline each below.

1. Water Resources and Inadequate Infrastructure
I am concerned that this development is being assessed for approval well ahead of the water and wastewater infrastructure needed to actually support it.
Sydney Water's own feasibility advice on this site states plainly that the existing drinking water system has very limited capacity to service the proposed development and may require significant trunk network upgrades, and that the sewer network servicing the site "was not designed to handle such a large flow." The servicing strategy for data centres in the Mamre Road precinct is under review due to known capacity constraints, with that review not due to complete until late 2026 — after this application is determined.
The proposal's stormwater management strategy is similarly reliant on a regional stormwater scheme that does not yet exist. The interim arrangements proposed (temporary detention tanks, local irrigation of undeveloped land, and a "diversion" of flows still being negotiated with Sydney Water) are stop-gap measures for a facility that will operate 24/7 for years before the permanent regional infrastructure is delivered.
I do not believe consent should be granted for a facility of this scale while: the local drinking water and sewer networks are acknowledged by Sydney Water to have inadequate capacity; the stormwater strategy depends on infrastructure that is not yet built, funded, or approved; and the precinct-wide servicing strategy for data centres specifically is still under active review.
Approving this application now pre-empts and undermines that review, and risks locking in infrastructure and environmental outcomes before the servicing strategy for the whole precinct has even been settled.
I also note that Penrith City Council's own submission to the NSW Parliamentary Inquiry on Data Centres highlights that Sydney's data centres could consume up to 25% of Sydney's total water demand by 2035 under high-growth scenarios. Precinct-wide, cumulative water demand from data centres in this area does not appear to have been adequately assessed in this application.

2. Air Quality
This facility includes 842 diesel backup generators and approximately 15,842 kilolitres of diesel fuel storage. This is an enormous concentration of diesel plant, located close to established residential areas and, critically, close to several schools and an aged care facility (Emmaus Catholic College, Trinity Catholic Primary School, Mamre Anglican School, and the Emmaus Retirement Village/Aged Care Facility).
I am concerned that:
Routine and system-level generator testing, as well as any mains power failure event triggering simultaneous operation of a large number of generators, will result in periodic but significant diesel emissions in close proximity to sensitive receivers, including children and elderly residents.
The NSW EPA raised extensive concerns on the original air quality assessment, finding that it failed to account for realistic operating scenarios beyond routine testing — including grid voltage disturbances, load shedding directed by the market operator, and possible grid-firming arrangements — and that it materially underestimated calm wind conditions in its meteorological modelling and understated likely generator operating hours.
Even the applicant's own revised assessment, finalised only weeks before this exhibition period, confirms that significant exceedances of the NO2, carbon monoxide and particulate matter (PM10/PM2.5) criteria are still predicted at residential receptors under emergency multi-generator operating scenarios. The applicant's response is not that these exceedances won't occur, but that the probability of the triggering power outage is low. I do not consider a "low probability" argument an adequate basis on which to approve a facility of this scale directly adjacent to schools and an aged care home.
Cumulative air quality modelling covering other existing, approved and proposed data centres in the Mamre Road Precinct was only added to the assessment after the EPA identified inconsistencies in how the applicant had modelled nearby facilities in the original submission. Given this history, I do not have confidence that the cumulative modelling now relied upon has been independently and rigorously verified.
I do not consider it acceptable for a development of this intensity — effectively hosting the diesel generation capacity of a small power station — to be approved directly adjacent to schools and an aged care facility without independently verified, worst-case air quality modelling covering all realistic failure and testing scenarios, and without binding, enforceable conditions limiting generator testing to times and durations that do not affect school and recreational hours.

3. Noise and Vibration
The proposal involves 24-hour, 7-day operation for an estimated 10-year construction period, plus decades of ongoing operational noise from cooling systems, electrical infrastructure and generator testing.
I am particularly concerned that:

The NSW EPA recommended an internal noise goal of 35 dB(A) for the nearby schools in the long-term scenario, consistent with standard protections for educational facilities. The applicant's assessment instead relies on a higher 40 dB(A) threshold, using a provision in the Noise Policy for Industry that applies where schools are already affected by existing industrial noise. Using an existing noise problem to justify a weaker standard for a new and larger noise source does not protect the acoustic amenity that students and aged care residents are entitled to.
Given the extended, roughly decade-long construction period, cumulative noise and "noise fatigue" impacts on nearby schools, the retirement village, and residents have not been given sufficient weight. NSW Health raised this same concern in its submission, specifically flagging risks of increased stress, sleep disruption, disruption to learning, and psychosocial impacts from prolonged, overlapping construction and operational noise.
Overlapping construction and operational noise (as later stages are built while earlier stages are already operating) has the potential to compound impacts on residents and sensitive receivers for years at a time, and I do not consider the proposed management measures sufficient to guarantee this will not cause real, ongoing harm to residents' quality of life, sleep, and wellbeing.

4. Energy Consumption and Grid Impact
This is a 1.2 gigawatt facility — an extraordinary amount of power for a single development, equivalent to a substantial fraction of the entire state's current data centre capacity. I am concerned about:
The impact of a load of this size on the reliability of the local electricity network and on electricity costs for surrounding residents, particularly during periods of peak demand or extreme heat, when Western Sydney is already vulnerable to strain on the grid.
The lack of a binding commitment, enforceable through consent conditions, that this facility will be powered by renewable energy sources rather than drawing on the general grid and effectively competing with local households and businesses for capacity.
The precedent this development sets for a precinct that is expected to accommodate multiple further data centres, without a clear, publicly available assessment of the cumulative power demand this land use is placing on the region.

5. Broader Environmental and Cumulative Impacts
Finally, I have broader concerns about the suitability of this scale of development on this site and in this location:

Council's own objection: Penrith City Council formally objected to this application on the grounds of the scale and suitability of the development for this site, and has separately raised concerns to the NSW Parliamentary Inquiry into Data Centres about the cumulative strain data centres are placing on local utilities, land use planning, and community amenity in Western Sydney.
Cumulative precinct-wide impact: This is not an isolated development. The Mamre Road Precinct already hosts (or has approved) multiple data centres, including a large Microsoft facility. I do not believe the cumulative noise, air quality, traffic, water and energy impacts of this concentration of data centre development in one precinct have been properly assessed anywhere in the application material.
Scale relative to planning controls: the proposal, even after amendment, still substantially exceeds the height limits generally anticipated for this precinct under the Mamre Road Precinct Development Control Plan, and represents an intensity of industrial use well beyond what the precinct's founding planning documents envisaged.

Conclusion
For the reasons set out above, I object to State Significant Development Application SSD-92743706. I ask that the Department and the Independent Planning Commission refuse the application for the reasons and concerns laid out, especially on the basis that essential water and wastewater servicing infrastructure does not yet exist, the energy consumption of data centres on our grid, and the precinct-wide data centre servicing strategy remains under review.
Maureen Sinclair

Generator testing regimes are designed to avoid school hours and peak outdoor activity periods

Consideration is given to minimising reliance on diesel generation over time, including transition pathways to lower-emission backup systems

Noise modelling specifically identifies predicted levels at the boundaries and learning areas of Emmaus Catholic College and Trinity Catholic Primary School

Tonal, low-frequency, and intermittent noise characteristics are appropriately assessed and mitigated

A clear framework for monitoring and responding to noise issues is implemented

Construction traffic management plans explicitly consider school peak periods, including morning drop-off and afternoon pick-up times

Hazard and risk assessments clearly demonstrate appropriate separation distances from sensitive land uses, including Emmaus Catholic College and Trinity Catholic Primary School
