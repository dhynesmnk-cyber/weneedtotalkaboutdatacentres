Executive Summary 
Introduction 
This Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) has been prepared by Mecone Group Pty Ltd (Mecone) on 
behalf of Goodman Property Services (Aust) Pty Limited (the Applicant) to accompany a State 
Significant Development Application (SSDA) for a 500 Mega Volt Amp (MVA) Data Centre at 10 
Roberts Road, Eastern Creek (the site).  
The EIS has been prepared to address the Secretary’s Environmental Assessment Requirements 
(SEARs) issued on the 16 January 2026 by the Planning Secretary under SSD-101067971 and has 
been certified by a registered environmental assessment practitioner (REAP).  
The proposed development is for the purpose of a Data Centre that has a total power consumption of 
more than 15 megawatts (348MW). Therefore, the project is declared State Significant Development 
(SSD) pursuant to Schedule 1, Clause 25 of State Environmental Planning Policy (Planning Systems) 
2021 (Planning Systems SEPP).  
This EIS has been prepared and submitted to the NSW Department of Planning, Housing and 
Infrastructure (DPHI) pursuant to Part 4 of the Environmental Planning and Assessment Act 1979 
(EP&A Act).  
The proposed development has been carefully designed to mitigate potential environmental impacts on 
the land and surrounding development while capitalising on the site’s strategic location to deliver critical 
digital infrastructure.  
Site Location and Context 
The proposed development is located at 10 Roberts Road, Eastern Creek, legally referred to as Lot 
553 DP1110447 with Offsite Enabling Infrastructure (OEI) proposed across Lot 21 DP1246626, Lot 20 
DP1157491, and Lot 22 DP1246626 to connect to TransGrid’s Sydney West Substation & Switchyard. 
The site is located on Country of the Dharug people within the local government area (LGA) of 
Blacktown.  
The Data Centre site has a land area of approximately 168,574m 2. The site has a primary frontage to 
Roberts Road which is a local road. Vehicle access is currently provided via Roberts Road.  
One existing warehouse building currently occupies the site with large expanses of hardstand areas for 
vehicle circulation and parking. Existing vegetation is mostly limited to landscape areas within setback 
areas.  
Surrounding land uses in all directions are predominantly industrial and commercial in nature. To the 
south, south-west and west of the site is land primarily owned and operated by TransGrid Sydney, 
inclusive of the OEI.   
The site is zoned IN1 General Industrial within Chapter 2 Western Sydney Employment Area of State 
Environmental Planning Policy (Industry and Employment) 2021 (IESEPP). The proposal is permissible 
with development consent in the IN1 zone pursuant to  the State Environmental Planning Policy 
(Transport and Infrastructure) 2021 (TISEPP).

Environmental Protection Licence 
Schedule 1 of the Protection of the Environment Operations Act 1997 (POEO Act) contains a core list 
of ‘scheduled activities’ that require a licence before they may be undertaken or carried out.  
Under the POEO Act 1997, ‘activity’ is defined as: 
“an industrial, agricultural or commercial activity or an activity of any other nature whatever 
(including the keeping of a substance or an animal).”  
Schedule 1, Part 1 of the POEO Act lists out scheduled activities for which an Environmental Protection 
Licence (EPL) is required for the premises at which it is carried out. The following scheduled activities 
of relevance to the proposed development are provided below: 
Schedule 1, Part 1, Clause 9 ‘Chemical Storage’ lists petroleum products storage as a scheduled 
activity, if the proposal includes: 
• General Chemical storage with capacity to store more than 20 tonnes (pressurised gases), 200
tonnes (liquefied gases) or 2,000 tonnes (chemicals in any other form), and
• Petroleum products storage with capacity to store more than 200 tonnes (liquefied gases) or
2,000 tonnes (chemicals in any other form)

1.4 Statutory Planning Context 
1.4.1 Environmental Planning and Assessment Act 1979 
The proposal has been assessed in accordance with the matters for consideration under Section 4.15 
of the EP&A Act 1979. The assessment concludes that the site is suitable for the proposed 
development and that the proposal is consistent with the public interest. 
Designated development is defined under Schedule 3, Part 2 of the Environmental Planning and 
Assessment Regulation 2021. While the proposal involves the storage of more than 2,000 tonnes of 
non-liquefied gas, Section 4.10(2) of the EP&A Act 1979 provides that designated development does 
not include State significant development. As the proposal constitutes State significant development, it 
is not classified as designated development. 
Integrated development is defined as development that requires the grant of consent from one or more 
public authorities for the development to be carried out. However, Section 4.46(1) of the EP&A Act 
1979 specifies that integrated development does not include State significant development or 
complying development. Accordingly, the proposal does not constitute integrated development.  
1.4.2 Protection of the Environment Operations Act 1997 
Schedule 1 of the Protection of the Environment Operations Act 1997 (POEO Act) contains a core list 
of ‘scheduled activities’ that require a licence before they may be undertaken or carried out.  
Under the POEO Act 1997, ‘activity’ is defined as:  
“an industrial, agricultural or commercial activity or an activity of any other nature whatever (including 
the keeping of a substance or an animal).”

of relevance to the proposed development are provided below: 
• Schedule 1, Part 1, Clause 9 ‘Chemical Storage’ lists petroleum products storage and general 
chemical storage as a scheduled activity, if the proposal includes:  
o Petroleum products storage - capacity to store more than 200 tonnes (liquefied gases)

3.5.1 Horizontal Directional Drilling (under boring) 
Horizontal Directional Drilling (HDD), also known as underboring, will be undertaken to install 
underground high voltage (HV) electrical conduits along part of connection corridor (refer to Figure 32). 
HDD is a trenchless construction methodology that enables the installation of conduits beneath roads, 
drainage infrastructure and other sensitive surface features without the need for open trench 
excavation. 
The HDD methodology as explained further below, has been selected to: 
• Minimise surface disturbance and vegetation removal 
• Avoid impacts to existing utilities and services 
• Maintain traffic movements where crossings are required 
• Reduce noise, dust and vibration compared to open trench construction 
• Avoid disturbance to waterways and stormwater infrastructure 
HDD Methodology 
The HDD construction process will comprise the following key stages: 
1. Entry and Exit Pits - Entry and exit pits will be excavated at either end of the bore alignment. 
These are anticipated to be approximately: 1.5 m (width) x 3 m (length) x 1.5 m (depth) 
2. Set-up of Equipment - Positioning of the drill rig and associated support equipment, including 
dewatering pumps, mixing and stabilisation units, and vacuum trucks. 
3. Pilot Drilling - A 150 mm diameter steerable drill head will be advanced along the designed 
bore path. The drill head will be continuously tracked using a transmitter to ensure accurate 
alignment. Drilling fluids will be used to stabilise the bore and facilitate removal of cuttings. 
4. Reaming - Upon completion of the pilot bore, the hole will be progressively enlarged to the 
required diameter (approximately 900 mm) using staged reaming (e.g. 300 mm, 450 mm, 600 
mm, up to 900 mm). Drilling fluids will continue to be used to maintain bore stability. 
5. Duct Installation and Grouting - Polyethylene Electrical (PE) conduits will be installed within 
the completed bore, followed by grouting of the annulus to ensure structural integrity and 
minimise voids. 
Biodiversity Mitigation Measures 
The concept design adopts a minimum drilling depth of approximately 4 metres below ground level 
beneath areas containing trees to be retained. 
This depth provides a substantial buffer relative to root zones, noting that: 
• AS 4970-2009 (Protection of Trees on Development Sites) identifies that boring beneath a TPZ 
may be undertaken at depths of 600 mm or greater, subject to arborist guidance 
[[page 73]]
 
 
 mecone.com.au | [email redacted] | 02 8667 8668 61 
 
• The proposed 4 m depth significantly exceeds this minimum, providing a conservative 
allowance to avoid structural roots and root plates 
To further validate this approach: 
• Root zone investigations and arboricultural assessment works are being undertaken, and 
• The final bore depth and alignment will be refined, where required, based on these findings to 
ensure impacts to tree roots are avoided 
3.5.2 Trenching 
Areas which are not required to be underbored will be trenched through conduits, which is a more 
simplified construction process than HDD. Two separate trenches would be excavated - each 
approximately 1 metre wide and up to 2 metres deep.  
Conduit installation would only require the opening of short sections of trench at a time (on average 
around 20 metres at any one location), with backfilling occurring as soon as each section of the 
conduits has been installed.  
Following the identification and relocation of services (if required), an excavator would be used to 
remove materials down to the base of the trench. Spoil would not be stockpiled at work sites but rather 
placed directly into trucks for transport to either the construction laydown areas for temporary storage 
or to an off-site appropriately licensed waste facility for disposal.  
An assessment would be made of the stability of the sides of the trench during excavation activities. 
Where necessary, shoring would be installed as a precaution against slump or collapse, particularly 
where deeper sections of trench are required. Barricades would be placed around open excavations 
whenever work is not being carried out at that location for an extended period of time.  
All works will be undertaken in accordance with applicable standards, environmental management 
plans, and approval conditions.

3.7 Cooling Systems  
The proposed cooling solution, direct-to-chip (DTC) liquid cooling supplemented by air cooling was 
selected for its targeted efficiency on high-heat-flux chips, ease of integration with existing air 
infrastructure, and scalability for 12MW halls, outperforming alternatives in retrofit-friendly Sydney Data 
Centres.  
The cooling system uses the following systems for each building: 
• Building 1: hybrid dry coolers for primary heat rejection, paired with air-cooled chiller trim 
featuring adiabatic pads (120 MW IT across 10 halls at 12 MW each, 26-36°C CHW 
temperature split)  
• Building 2: water-cooled chiller trim for Building 2 (180 MW IT across 15 halls + 48 IT MW 
across 4 halls, 30-40°C CHW temperature split).  
The system represents the most energy-efficient cooling solution for high-power density Data Centre 
and will achieve an annual average PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness) of 1.25 and annual water 
utilisation effectiveness (WUE) of 1.47 across both buildings. 
There is a total of 16N Fan Wall Units (FWUs) serving each Data Hall in Building 1 and 14N FWUs 
serving each Data Hall in Building 2 in an (N+4) redundancy configuration. Each FWU has a capacity of 
337 kW (BLD1) and 385 kW (BLD2). Air flow rate is designed at 75l/s/kW IT. FWUs are provided by 
dual fed ups backed power supply. 
The hybrid design supports future retrofits through modular HDC scalability for emerging technologies, 
with no open towers to avoid plume/legionella risks per AS/NZS 3666. 
Maintenance 
The cooling systems are continuously monitored for failure and performance. Maintenance is 
undertaken on each Fan Wall Unit separately. As N+2 minimum redundancy is provided, the loss of two 
(2) units does not affect the cooling function.  
In the event of a power failure, the cooling is restored within 12 to 20 seconds, i.e. the time taken for 
the generators to operate. 5 minutes of thermal storage is provided for generator start-up time and 
chiller ramp-up time. 24 hours of water storage is provided for the heat rejection plant in the event of 
mains water failure.

A full-time technical team will be on site, continually monitoring the operation of the cooling systems. A 
comprehensive regular testing and maintenance programme is performed monthly on the cooling 
systems to ensure their continuous operation. Due to the critical nature of the facility and reliance upon 
cooling systems for the Data processing, maintenance is especially important.

3.9.2 Security  
The security framework for Project Atlas is established in accordance with the Goodman security 
protocols, utilising a tiered approach that balances site permeability with rigorous asset protection.  
The campus is organized into four primary security gradients:  
• Public Access: Managed via the primary vehicle turnaround and entry thresholds.  
• Substation Access: A secondary security layer providing controlled entry for specialised 
maintenance and utility providers.  
• Site Access: A hardened perimeter ensuring a secure transition from the site boundary to the 
inner campus with 2.4m high outer and inner fencing.  
• Building Access: The final external tier, defined by the building’s physical envelope and facility 
outline.  
Beyond these external demarcations, the internal architecture provides additional layers of security, 
ensuring that the critical digital infrastructure remains resilient and protected from unauthorised access.

6.1 Built Form and Design 
6.1.1 Design and Layout 
The proposed design and layout respond to the land’s inherent characteristics, leveraging existing 
infrastructure and topography to optimise operational security and environmental performance. By 
integrating the facility into the natural levels of the site, the proposal minimises the need for substantial 
earthworks and superficial screening, instead using the site’s physical profile to provide inherent 
privacy and buffering. 
The design principles adopted by the proposed development aim to enhance both the immediate 
context and the broader Erskine Park Industrial Precinct as explained below: 
• Operational efficiencies: The internal layout of the proposed Data Centre has largely been 
driven by the stringent design and operational requirements of the Data Centre. The 
architects and the engineers worked closely to arrive at a compact and efficient, 
programmatic design that maximises the use of space, which can meet the operational 
needs of end users. 
• Building Orientation: The two Data Centre buildings have been oriented to the east along 
Roberts Road and west towards Old Wallgrove Road with substantial building setbacks to 
each lot boundary. 
[[page 100]]
 
 
 mecone.com.au | [email redacted] | 02 8667 8668 88 
 
• Vehicle Movements: An internal circulation ring has been designed to provide clearly 
defined and efficient servicing access to multiple loading docks with separate access 
dedicated to substation access. 
• Back of House – Essential components—including pumps, industrial water tanks, retention 
systems, and diesel storage—are strategically located within the building forms to mitigate 
visual and acoustic impacts. Additionally, loading bays are positioned for immediate 
accessibility from the primary circulation ring, ensuring seamless equipment replacement and 
servicing without disrupting site operations.  
• Site Servicing: 15–20m electrical easements are integrated to facilitate robust reticulation 
from the substation, supporting both immediate demand and future scalability across the 
precinct.  
• Security: To meet the stringent requirements of critical digital infrastructure, a secure zone 
defined by a 2.4m primary outer boundary, reinforced by a secondary 2.1m internal security 
line is required for the site.  
• Landscaping: Existing tree lines are maintained and reinforced where possible along the 
northwest and southwest boundaries to bolster site privacy and contribute to the 
neighbouring biodiversity corridor.  
 
6.1.2 Bulk and Scale  
The site is located within an established industrial precinct, characterised by large-format industrial 
buildings including logistics and Data Centres. Grimshaw and project engineers have worked closely to 
arrive at a compact, efficient and programmatic massing that is holistically considered and compatible 
with developments in the surrounding context.  
The proposal comprises two Data Centre buildings, with heights of 24.02m and 34.8m. The buildings 
are conceptualised with a series of discernible linear volumes that express their underlying 
programmatic functions, enabling an honest expression of the building. Exposed structural elements 
and a nuanced facade types that balance economies of scale with the imperative to mitigate structural 
mass. This facilitates a sophisticated volumetric articulation and a meaningful dialogue between the building 
forms and their urban context.  
The building base is articulated at a datum relative to the human scale, fostering a more intimate and 
navigable street-level experience. By utilising the tectonics of the fire stairs and expressed structural 
modules, the design is able to create visual depth and interest. 
Overall, the development is consistent with the bulk and scale of the surrounding precinct and its built 
form is a direct design response to its function.

6.3 Hazards and Risks 
A Preliminary Risks and Hazards Analysis (PHA) has been prepared by Core Engineering Group at 
Appendix S. The aim of the PHA is to identify risks applicable to the site that are not within the 
acceptable levels laid out within the PHA and apply mitigating controls reach to an acceptable level. 
The Data Centre is proposed to contain the following hazardous materials stored on site:  
• 2,994,390kg of lithium-ion (li-ion) batteries,  
• 4,100,00L of combustible liquid (Diesel).  
Diesel storage will comprise external bulk tanks between Buildings 1 and 2 and day tanks within each 
generator enclosure. 37 bulk tanks are proposed, either below ground or on-grade, supplying the day 
tanks via fixed pumps. Day tanks provide short-term fuel and are located from ground to Level 2 in 
Building 1 and ground to Level 3 in Building 2. 
Data Centres typically use battery backup systems in dedicated battery rooms, in server racks, or a 
combination of both. The final configuration will depend on the customer, with some halls potentially 
using in-rack batteries instead of separate battery rooms, though overall battery quantities onsite are 
expected to remain similar. Associated hazards and mitigation measures will be addressed in the 
[[page 108]]
 
 
 mecone.com.au | [email redacted] | 02 8667 8668 96 
 
report. Small numbers of spare or replaced batteries will be stored onsite, but these are negligible 
compared to total quantities. 
Preliminary Risk Screening 
SEPP 33 provides guidance for performing a preliminary risk screening for dangerous goods within 
developments. However, Table 1 of the SEPP 33 screening methods does not specify methods for 
Class C1, C2, or Class 9 dangerous goods. Consequently, following the SEPP 33 guidelines alone 
does not identify any requirement for additional risk screening for the development based on the 
proposed dangerous goods. Notwithstanding this, Appendix 7 of SEPP 33 sets threshold quantities that 
trigger obligations to notify WorkCover NSW (now SafeWork NSW) and prepare a manifest and 
emergency plan.  
The proposed quantities of dangerous goods on the site exceed these thresholds, activating specific 
regulatory requirements. 
The assessment considered risks associated with potential human fatalities, property damage, and 
environmental pollution. Where necessary, hazard treatment measures have been proposed to reduce 
risks to an ‘As Low As Reasonably Practicable’ (ALARP) level, in line with the risk acceptance criteria 
described in Section 6.1 of the PHA.

are to be integrated into the design and construction of the site.

The proposal includes provision for at-grade 150 car parking spaces on the site. It has been noted by 
Ason Group that neither the ECPP nor the Guide to Transport Impact Assessment (GTIA) provide 
parking demand rates for Data Centres. Therefore, a first principles assessment has been undertaken 
based on the operational information provided. The following are the number of staff expected to be on-
site:  
• Engineering Operations: 92 staff (46 staff across 2 shifts) 
• Operations: 70 staff 
Noting the above, the development will employ up to 162 staff, however it is critical to understand the 
staff travel patterns to understand the parking demand likely to be generated by the site. 
It is understood that the entire operations team comprising 70 staff will be on-site in one shift which is 
expected to be during standard office hours. However, the engineering operations team will be on-site 
across 2 shifts with the shift changeover to occur when the operations team is no longer on-site. 
Therefore, it is anticipated that the maximum parking demand to be generated when the operations 
team and engineering operations team are both on-site is 116 car parks. 
Based on factors including limited public transport accessibility to the Site and distance from residential 
areas, it is expected that a conservative application of 1 car parking space per on-site staff would be an 
appropriate provision of car parking. 
It should be noted that there would be staff that will travel to the site via alternative modes of transport 
(i.e. public and/or active transport) and that due to the locality, it is envisaged that some staff may opt to 
carpool to travel to the site. Therefore, the application of 1 car parking space per on-site staff is 
considered a robust assessment to ensure that the absolute peak parking demand can be 
accommodated wholly within the site whilst also providing flexibility for occasional parking demand 
generated by external factors such as maintenance workers and visitors.

Avoid and Minimise Impacts 
The site selection avoids areas of high biodiversity value, as far as practicable, and represents a 
strategic reuse of previously disturbed land, consistent with BAM’s emphasis on minimising impacts 
through informed site selection.  
Alternative sites were considered for the Data Centre development during the selection and due 
diligence process and were ultimately discounted due to key constraints, including existing customer 
tenure, increased distance from the bulk supply point (which would require additional infrastructure and 
greater land disturbance), and the presence of structurally sound buildings with substantial remaining 
service life.  
In contrast, the building within the data centre site has reached the end of its functional life and is 
suitable for redevelopment. The proposed site was therefore identified as the most appropriate location, 
enabling the project to meet operational requirements while minimising unnecessary environmental 
impacts and avoiding direct impact to mapped biodiversity features (CPW and threatened species 
habitats).  
The OEI pathway has been planned, as far as practicably possible, to avoid areas of high biodiversity 
value. The cable pathway will be trenched within the existing BESS driveway and road reserve areas, 
whilst sections intersecting native vegetation will be underbored to a suitable depth to avoid impacts on 
overhead trees and vegetation. 
Potential impacts to threatened species due to clearing of planted native vegetation and habitat 
connectivity will be minimised by the retention and protection of a large strip of existing trees along the 
western boundary of the data centre site as outlined by the Arborist report (Appendix BB).  
Various flora and fauna management measures (such as pre-clearance surveys and monitoring) are 
proposed to minimise or mitigate direct and indirect impacts on threatened species associated with

6.8 Noise  
A Noise and Vibration Impact Assessment (NVIA) has been prepared by Renzo and Tonin and is 
provided Appendix I. The assessment considers the potential noise and vibration impacts that may 
result from the construction and operation phases of the development. 
Existing Environments 
Noise monitoring was undertaken at four residential locations in Minchinbury, Horsley Park and Erskine 
Park over several weeks to understand the existing noise conditions in the area surrounding the 
proposed Data Centre. The monitoring used certified Class 1 noise loggers positioned in front or back 
yards and measured noise continuously during the day, evening and night.  
The existing noise environment is largely shaped by nearby major roads. In Minchinbury, the Western 
Motorway is the dominant source of noise, creating generally higher background levels. In Horsley 
Park, noise conditions are quieter overall, influenced mainly by the M7 and occasional industrial 
activity. In Erskine Park, the main contributor is traffic along Lenore Drive, combined with general 
neighbourhood sounds such as dogs barking and birds. Background noise levels typically ranged from 
around 35–47 dB(A) across the monitored locations, consistent with normal suburban and semi-rural 
environments. These measured baseline levels form the basis for setting appropriate noise limits for 
construction and operation of the proposed Data Centre. 
Potential Impacts 
The assessment follows the NSW EPA Noise Policy for Industry (NPfI), which requires two key checks: 
ensuring the new noise does not exceed background noise by more than 5 dB (intrusive criteria), and 
ensuring the overall noise in the area remains within acceptable limits for the type of neighbourhood 
(amenity criteria). To do this, the report establishes project noise trigger levels (PNTLs) for all nearby 
receivers, including residential areas in Horsley Park, Erskine Park and Minchinbury, as well as the 
nearby childcare centre and industrial neighbours. These limits are based on the noise monitoring 
presented in the NVIA 
The modelling considered both typical operations and worst-case scenarios, including simultaneous 
operation of multiple cooling units and the routine testing of up to five emergency generators at once. 
With the recommended mitigation measures included in the design, such as acoustic louvres, upgraded 
generator enclosures, noise-controlled transformers, and sound-absorbing materials inside plant 
rooms, the predicted operational noise levels comply with all PNTLs at every surrounding sensitive 
receiver.  
The assessment also checked for “annoying” noise characteristics such as tonality or low-frequency 
noise and applied conservative penalties where needed. Even under these conditions, predicted noise 
remained within required limits.  
A detailed sleep-disturbance assessment was also completed. The highest predicted maximum noise 
levels at night (LAmax) for nearby homes remained well below the thresholds at which people are likely 
to wake, meaning the Data Centre is not expected to disturb sleep. The assessment further notes that 
emergency generator operation during an actual power outage is extremely rare and therefore no strict 
noise limits are applied to those events, although predicted levels were still reported for reference.  
The NVIA includes an assessment of noise from operational traffic. Because the Data Centre 
generates only modest traffic volumes and nearby roads are already busy, the increase in road traffic

mean that construction noise impacting a sensitive receiver may be louder than from an individual set

6.17.2 Vibration  
An assessment of construction vibration impacts for the project has been completed by Renzo and 
Tonin as Appendix I. As a screening assessment, items potentially impacted by vibration were 
identified if they were located within worst case minimum working distances from the various proposed 
vibration intensive activities, for the worst-case structure/receiver type. 
The results show that the predicted vibration levels generated by typical construction plant (such as 
excavators, rollers and trucks) are well below the thresholds associated with structural damage for both 
residential and industrial buildings. The assessment applies internationally recognised criteria, including 
BS 7385 for cosmetic building damage and DIN 4150-3 for sensitive or structurally unsound structures. 
Based on the minimum working distances modelled, vibration-intensive activities (such as the use of a 
smooth drum roller or bored piling) are not expected to generate levels capable of causing cosmetic or 
structural damage at any nearby receivers.  
The report also evaluates the potential for human annoyance from vibration, using criteria from the 
NSW Assessing Vibration Technical Guideline. The predicted vibration levels at all nearby residential 
and non-residential receptors are significantly lower than the levels where occupants might experience 
discomfort, meaning human annoyance impacts are not expected. In addition, the assessment confirms 
that buildings housing vibration-sensitive data-centre equipment located near the construction site are 
also unlikely to experience any adverse vibration effects, with predicted levels sitting well below the 
conservative screening limits for such equipment

The following mitigation measures are proposed to address potential impacts from vibration during 
construction: 
• To prevent cosmetic building damage 
o Maintain minimum working distances for vibration-intensive equipment such as rollers 
and piling rigs  
o Use alternative, lower-vibration construction methods where feasible if works must occur 
closer than the recommended distances 
o Implement vibration monitoring when operating within screening distances, especially 
near sensitive or older structures.  
o Modify work practices (e.g., reduce power, alter sequencing, change plant) if monitoring 
indicates vibration levels approaching thresholds 
• To minimise human annoyance 
o Schedule vibration-intensive activities only during daytime hours to reduce disturbance 
to residents.  
o Avoid running multiple high-vibration machines at the same time, where practicable.  
o Provide advance notification to nearby occupants before high-vibration works occur.  
o Ensure workers follow site induction procedures, including best-practice handling of

• To protect vibration-sensitive equipment 
o Identify any nearby buildings containing vibration-sensitive equipment (e.g., data-centre 
or telecoms equipment).  
o Use site-specific vibration monitoring if works occur near these facilities.  
o Confirm equipment-specific vibration limits, and apply stricter controls where required.

6.19.2 Water Sensitive Urban Design 
Reduced Water Consumption 
The cooling system is designed to take advantage of a waterside economy cycle / free cooling via 
hybrid coolers, bypassing chiller operation to reduce energy consumption. When ambient conditions 
allow, the hybrid coolers and chillers can operate in dry mode, further minimising water usage. 
The project will also adopt highly efficient water fixtures with WELS ratings and appliances for use. 
Recycled Water and Reuse 
Due to the high-water demand of the evaporative cooling equipment particularly during periods of low 
rainfall, tank sizes larger than 300kL do not yield a significant increase in the amount of rainwater being 
used. Increasing the catchment area that is discharging to the tank does yield an improvement in the 
amount of rainwater being utilised throughout the year, but it significantly increases the amount of 
rainwater overflowing out of the tank. 
As such, a 300kL rainwater harvesting tank is therefore considered suitable in minimising non-potable 
water demand for the project.  
Strategic Water Metering   
Building-level and sub-metering will be provided to monitor and record the water consumption of the 
overall development, as well as irrigation systems, water fixtures and water use for the mechanical 
systems.

A Greenhouse Gas Emissions Report (GHG) has been prepared by LCI at Appendix N and supported 
by an independent expert review completed by Zephyr Environmental at Appendix PP in accordance

The GHG report has been prepared to address the requirements applicable to the project as defined in 
the NSW Guide for Large Emitters developed by the Environment Protection Authority (EPA). 
The assessment confirms that the project meets the Guide’s large-emitter criteria, particularly the 
likelihood of exceeding 25,000 tCO₂-e/year of Scope 1 and 2 emissions during operation, and therefore 
requires a full GHG assessment, emissions estimation across Scopes 1–3, and the application of the 
mitigation hierarchy. The assessment evaluates construction and operational emissions across three 
scenarios: Benchmark, Design Measures, and All Measures, with the last aligning with the applicant’s 
net-zero transition strategy. 
Mitigation Measures 
1. Avoid and Reduce  
The following energy efficient design features considered best-practice are being pursued for the 
design to support the Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) target of 1.25 annual average:  
• Elevated data hall cooling setpoint temperature and relaxed humidity setpoints combined with 
direct to chip liquid cooling capability to maximise efficiency of mechanical plant  
• Waterside economy cycle / free cooling from hybrid dry coolers to the bulk of the data hall when 
ambient conditions allow bypassing chiller operation to further reduce energy consumption  
• High efficiency chiller plant through modern compressor technology coupled with elevated 
operational chilled water temperatures due to relaxed hall conditions and direct to chip liquid 
cooling  
• On-site PV maximised to the available roof space with a targeted rated output capacity in the order 
of a minimum 1,200 kWe across the site.  
• High efficiency LED lighting selections throughout to minimise demand coupled with occupant 
responsive controls to minimise operational time and associated energy consumption  
• Low absorptivity / high Solar Reflective Index (SRI) building external finishes to reduce radiant heat 
gains
