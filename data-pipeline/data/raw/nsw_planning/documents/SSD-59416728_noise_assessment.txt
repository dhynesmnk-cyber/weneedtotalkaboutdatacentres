Executive summary 
This report presents an operational and construction noise and vibration impact assessment of the proposed 
development located at 3 Davis Road, Wetherill Park, NSW. It addresses the following acoustic design aspects in 
response to the industry-specific SEARs for the project: 
▪ Operational noise and vibration emissions and impact  
▪ Construction noise and vibration emissions and impact 
▪ Noise and vibration mitigation and management measures  
Potential noise impacts from the proposed development have been assessed with reference to relevant requirements of 
the following: 
▪ NSW Protection of the Environment Operations Act 1997 (POEO Act). 
▪ NSW EPA Noise Policy for Industry 2017 (NPfI) 
▪ NSW Interim Construction Noise Guideline (ICNG) 
▪ Transport for NSW Construction Noise and Vibration Strategy 
▪ NSW Department of Environment and Conservation (DEC) guideline Assessing Vibration: a technical guideline 
Noise emissions from the key identified noise sources associated with the development were assessed with appropriate 
noise control measures recommended to meet the relevant project assessment noise criteria. Noise and vibration impact 
from the construction of the project were also considered with management measures recommended to assist with 
control of impacts during project construction. Based on the assessment undertaken in this report, it is concluded that 
potential noise impacts from the development can be adequately controlled to achieve compliance with relevant noise 
criteria and requirements subject to incorporation of acoustic recommendations and mitigation measures in this report.

1.0 Introduction 
Cundall has been commissioned to prepare a noise and vibration impact assessment for the proposed development 
located at 3 Davis Road, Wetherill Park, NSW. The proposed development comprises of two three storey datacentre 
buildings (DCs), a two storey tape storage building, a high voltage substation (HVSS) and associated infrastructure and 
amenities.  
The proposed development site is located at 3 Davis Road Wetherill Park (legally described as Lot 1 DP864615) within 
the Smithfield-Wetherill Park Industrial Estate of the Fairfield City Council (Council) Local Government Area (LGA) and is 
zoned E4 General Industrial. The development site has a total land area of approximately 105,400 m2 with 360m of 
frontage to Davis Road. 
The proposed development is classified as State Significant Development (SSD) under the provisions of the State 
Environmental Planning Policy (Planning Systems) 2021 and is subject to the industry-specific Secretary’s 
Environmental Assessment Requirements (SEARs). 
A noise and vibration assessment is required under the provisions of the SEARs to address potential construction and 
operational noise and vibration impacts including testing of any backup generators on surrounding noise sensitive 
receivers and outline any relevant noise and vibration management measures. 
This report considers the potential noise and vibration impacts associated with the following aspects of the proposed 
development addressing the relevant acoustic requirements of the SEARs: 
▪ Operational noise and vibration emissions and impact  
▪ Construction noise and vibration emissions and impact 
▪ Noise and vibration mitigation and management measures  
Construction and operational noise and vibration impacts associated with the proposed development are assessed in 
accordance with the provisions of the following documents: 
▪ NSW Protection of the Environment Operations Act 1997 (POEO Act). 
▪ NSW EPA Noise Policy for Industry 2017 (NPfI) 
▪ NSW Interim Construction Noise Guideline (ICNG) 
▪ Transport for NSW Construction Noise and Vibration Strategy 
▪ NSW Department of Environment and Conservation (DEC) guideline Assessing Vibration: a technical guideline

4.0 Operational noise and vibration 
4.1 Operational noise criteria 
4.1.1 NSW Noise Policy for Industry 
Environmental noise emissions from the proposed development should be designed to comply with the requirements of 
the NSW EPA Noise Policy for Industry 2017 (NPfI).  
The objective of the NPfI is to ensure noise impacts from the proposed developments are assessed and managed in a 
consistent and transparent manner. If it is predicted that the development is likely to cause the project noise trigger level 
to be exceeded at noise sensitive receivers, management measures need to be considered to seek to reduce the 
potential noise impacts.  
It should be noted that NPfI applies to the assessment of industrial and commercial noise sources and vehicle 
movements within the premises, which in this case would relate to mechanical services plant, routine testing of the 
generators and site vehicle movement noise (i.e. loading dock vehicles and car parking).    
The project noise trigger level provides an objective for assessing a proposal or site. It is not intended for use as a 
mandatory requirement. The project noise trigger level is a level that, if exceeded, would indicate a potential noise impact 
on the community, and so ‘trigger’ a management response; for example, further investigation of mitigation measures.  
The project noise trigger level, feasible and reasonable mitigation measures, and consideration of residual noise impacts 
are used together to assess noise impact and manage the potential noise from the site.  
The project noise trigger level is the lower (that is, the more stringent) value of the project intrusiveness noise level and 
project amenity noise level. The project intrusiveness noise level aims to protect against significant changes in noise 
levels, whilst the project amenity noise level seeks to protect against cumulative noise impacts from industry and 
maintain amenity for particular land uses. Applying the most stringent requirement as the project noise trigger level 
ensures that both intrusive noise is limited and amenity is protected, and that no single development can unacceptably 
increase the noise level within an area. The NPfI separates the day into three different time periods – daytime, evening 
and night-time. These time periods are detailed below in Table 4-1.

LAeq, 15min = RBL + 5 dB

from the proposed development. Criteria consider the type of receiver, the area classification and the time of day the 
noise is proposed to occur. The amenity criterion is set so that the LAeq noise level from the industrial noise source does 
not increase the total industrial noise levels at the receiver above the recommended Amenity Noise Level (ANL) for that 
receiver. Project amenity noise level is defined as: 
Project amenity noise level = ANL - 5 dB(A) 
where ANL is the recommended Amenity Noise Level established in accordance with Table 2.2 of the NPfI based on 
noise amenity area of the receivers. In cases where no other industries are present in the area, the project amenity noise 
level is set to the ANL for the proposed development. Exceptions apply to above for exceptional cases such as areas 
with high traffic. Neither of these scenarios are applicable and hence not further discussed in this document.

4.2 Noise model 
4.2.1 Methodology 
A 3D computational noise model of facility and surrounding has been developed and utilised for assessment of noise 
emissions from the facility using SoundPLAN Version 9.0 and adopting ISO 9613 -2:1996 Attenuation of sound during 
propagation outdoors algorithm.  
Computational noise model has been utilised to calculate outdoor noise propagation from different noise sources taking 
into consideration the building and facility design, surrounding building, receivers location and height and terrain as well 
as environmental factors affecting the noise propagation. The noise predictions utilise ISO 9613 algorithm which takes 
into account a moderately favourable weather conditions to the noise propagation (i.e. increasing noise levels). These 
include downwind propagation (approximate wind speeds of 1 – 5 m/s at a height of 3 – 11 m above ground) or 
equivalently propagation under a well-developed moderate ground-based temperature inversion such as commonly 
occurs at night. 3D visual representation of the developed computational model of the site and surrounding areas are 
shown in Figure 4-1 below.

to account for any potential noise characters associated with the noise from the development.

5.2.6 Construction noise management 
5.2.6.1 Feasible and reasonable  
Application of the feasible and reasonable measures and work practices should be considered at all times by the project. 
A work practice or management measure is considered feasible where it can be engineered and is practical to build 
given the project constraints such as safety, maintenance and reliability requirements.  
Implementation of reasonable measures from those feasible involve judging whether the overall noise benefits outweigh 
the overall adverse social, economic and environmental effects, including the cost of the mitigation measure. The 
following are key factors taking into account when assessing reasonableness of a measure/work practice:  
▪ Noise impacts such as existing acoustic environment, projected changes, number of people affected or duration of 
exposure 
▪ Noise mitigation benefits such as reductions expected and number of people benefited. 
▪ Cost effectiveness of measure such as total cost, comparison with total project costs, disruption to essential 
infrastructure, risk to community or worker safety 
▪ Community views such as engagement with affected receivers, engagement with wider community, consideration of 
measures that have majority support from community.  
5.2.6.2 Specific noise mitigation measures 
The following specific noise management measures are recommended: 
▪ Incorporation of construction noise barriers around the site boundary with a minimum height of 3 m to block direct 
line of sight between noisy construction sources and activities and adjoining industrial premises.  
▪ Construction noise barrier should achieve a minimum surface density of 10 kg/m2 where practical and have no gaps 
on the face or bottom.

5.3 Construction vibration 
5.3.1 Structural damage vibration criteria 
Most commonly specified “safe” structural vibration limits are designed to minimise the risk of threshold or cosmetic 
surface cracks and are set well below the levels that have potential to cause damage to the main structure. Example of 
these vibration level limits are nominated within the BBS7385: Part 2. 
The vibration criteria specified within the DIN 4150: Part 3 have been recommended for structures surrounding the 
project site. The criteria specified within DIN 4150 are designed for controls of continuous long-term vibration or repetitive 
vibration with the potential to cause fatigue effects to structure.   
The following Peak Particle Velocity (PPV) values are specified within DIN 4150 as safe limits, below which even 
superficial cosmetic damage is not to be expected: 
▪ 10 mm/s for commercial buildings and buildings of similar design. 
▪ 5 mm/s for dwellings and buildings or similar design. 
▪ 2.5 mm/s for buildings of great intrinsic value (e.g. heritage listed buildings).

Appendix A - Acoustic terminology 
ASSESSMENT BACKGROUND LEVEL (ABL)  
A single-number figure used to characterise the background noise levels from a single day of a noise survey. ABL is 
derived from the measured noise levels for the day, evening or night-time period of a single day of background 
measurements. The ABL is calculated to be the tenth percentile of the background LA90 noise levels – i.e., the 
measured background noise is above the ABL 90% of the time.  
‘A’-WEIGHTED SOUND LEVEL dBA  
The unit generally used for measuring environmental, traffic or industrial noise is the A-weighted sound pressure level in 
decibels, denoted dBA. An A-weighting network can be built into a sound level measuring instrument such that sound 
levels in dBA can be read directly from a meter. The weighting is based on the frequency response of the human ear 
and has been found to correlate well with human subjective reactions to various sounds. An increase or decrease of 
approximately 10 dB corresponds to a subjective doubling or halving of the loudness of a noise. A change of 2 to 3 dB is 
subjectively barely perceptible.  
DECIBEL  
The ratio of sound pressures which we can hear is a ratio of one million to one. For convenience, therefore, a logarithmic 
measurement scale is used. The resulting parameter is called the ‘sound level’ (L) and the associated measurement unit 
is the decibel (dB). As the decibel is a logarithmic ratio, the laws of logarithmic addition and subtraction apply. 
EQUIVALENT CONTINUOUS SOUND LEVEL (LAeq)  
Another index for assessment for overall noise exposure is the equivalent continuous sound level, Leq. This is a notional 
steady level, which would, over a given period of time, deliver the same sound energy as the actual time-varying sound 
over the same period. Hence fluctuating levels can be described in terms of a single figure level.  
FREQUENCY  
The rate of repetition of a sound wave. The subjective equivalent in music is pitch. The unit of frequency is the Hertz 
(Hz), which is identical to cycles per second. A thousand hertz is often denoted kilohertz (kHz), e.g., 2 kHz = 2000 Hz. 
Human hearing ranges from approximately 20 Hz to 20 kHz. The most commonly used frequency bands are octave 
bands, in which the mid frequency of each band is twice that of the band below it. For design purposes, the octave 
bands between 63 Hz to 8 kHz are generally used.  
For more detailed analysis, each octave band may be split into three one-third octave bands or, in some cases, narrower 
frequency bands.  
RATING BACKGROUND LEVEL (RBL)  
A single-number figure used to characterise the background noise levels from a complete noise survey.  
The RBL for a day, evening or night-time period for the overall survey is calculated from the individual Assessment 
Background Levels (ABL) for each day of the measurement period, and is numerically equal to the median (middle 
value) of the ABL values for the days in the noise survey.  
SOUND POWER AND SOUND PRESSURE  
The sound power level (Lw) of a source is a measure of the total acoustic power radiated by a source. 
The sound pressure level (Lp) varies as a function of distance from a source. However, the sound power level is an 
intrinsic characteristic of a source (analogous to its mass), which is not affected by the environment within which the 
source is located.

STATISTICAL NOISE LEVELS  
For levels of noise that vary widely with time, for example road traffic noise, it is necessary to employ an index that allows 
for this variation. ‘A’-weighted statistical noise levels are denoted LA10, dB LA90 etc. The reference time period (T) is 
normally included, e.g., dB LA10, 5min or dB LA90,8hr.  
LA90 (T)  
Refers to the sound pressure level measured in dBA, exceeded for 90% of the time interval (T) – i.e., measured noise 
levels were greater than this value for 90% of the time interval. This is also often referred to the background noise level.  
LA10 (T)  
Refers to the sound pressure level measured in dBA, exceeded for 10% of the time interval (T). This is often referred to 
as the average maximum noise level and is frequently used to describe traffic noise.
