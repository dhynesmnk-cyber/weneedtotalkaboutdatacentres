Executive Summary 
INTRODUCTION 
This Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) has been prepared by Patch Planning (Patch) on 
behalf of Lehr Consultants International (Australia) Pty Ltd (the Applicant) in support of a 
State Significant Development Application (SSDA) for a new 96-megawatt (MW) data centre 
at the site known as 6 Honeman Close, Huntingwood. 
The proposal has a capital investment value (CIV) of $1,263,226,278.52 exc. GST and will result 
in the provision of critical infrastructure and represents a significant private investment in 
New South Wales, which contribute to continued economic prosperity.  
The EIS has been prepared to respond to S ecretary’s Environmental Assessment 
Requirements (SEARs) received under SSD-58601963 and has been certified by a registered 
environmental assessment practitioner (REAP). The SSDA is submitted to the Department of 
Planning and Environment (DPE) under Part 4 of the Environmental Planning and 
Assessment Act 1979 (EP&A Act 1979). 
LOCATION AND SITE DESCRIPTION 
The SSDA relates to a proposal at the site known as 6 Honeman Close, Huntingwood, legally 
referred to as Lot 5 DP1238405. The site is within the Blacktown City Council local 
government area (LGA) and is bound by the Great Western Highway to the north, a small 
extent of land fronting Reservoir Road to the west, and Honeman Close to the south and 
west. The subject site is approximately 5.04 hectares in size and irregularly shaped.  
Development in the surrounds varies in nature. To the north, beyond the Great Western 
Highway, development comprises a range of light industrial, warehousing, and logistic 
development types, while to the northeast, a residential estate known as ‘The Palms’  is 
located. To the south, the site is bound by Honeman Close, which is a state and locally listed 
heritage item. Beyond Honeman Close there is undeveloped industrial-zoned land. To the 
west, land comprises undeveloped industrial-zoned land as well as a Transport for New South 
Wales (TfNSW) facility known as the ‘Crash Lab’. Beyond this, the broader Huntingwood 
Estate is located. Land to the east, beyond Reservoir Road, comprises a Bunnings Warehouse. 
The site falls from a high point of approximately RL 76 in the south eastern corner of the site 
(near Reservoir Road) to approximately RL 59 in the north western corner (at the Great 
Western Highway). 
Currently, much of the site has been cleared of vegetation, except for land at the northwest 
of the site. 
RELATED RELEVANT APPROVALS 
On 28 June 2019, conditional consent was granted in relation to the site in the Land and 
Environment Court (LEC) for a two -stage concept development application. Originally 
assessed by Council under DA/17/01780, the LEC judgement provided approval for Stage 1 
Torrens title subdivision into three lots, demolition of existing structures, tree removal, and 
construction of a service station (with associate d car wash, convenience store and cafe). In 
addition, road works, landscaping, drainage, and earthworks are also approved under the 
consent. 
Tree clearing associated with DA/17/01780 has recently been undertaken.

PROJECT SUMMARY 
The subject proposal relates to a new 5 storey 96MW data centre facility which will operate 
24 hours a day, 7 days a week. In summary, approval for the following is sought:  
• Partial clearing of the site of vegetation (where not already approved under DA -17-
01780); 
• Site preparation works and earthworks and installation of services to enable the 
construction of the data centre; 
• Construction of a 5 storey 96MW data centre facility including associated mechanical, 
electrical and water infrastructure, along with 4 6 diesel generators required in order 
to ensure the proper and safe functioning of the facility; 
• Construction of a vehicular access from Reservoir Road, internal roads, loading areas 
and on-site car parking; 
• Landscaping works, including  new and replacement planting throughout the site ; 
and  
• The establishment of a conservation area located to the west of the site  with a total 
area of 0.78 hectares (ha) and subsequent subdivision. 
PROJECT ALTERNATIVES 
Under Clause 192 of Environmental Planning and Assessment Regulation 2021  (EP&A 
Regulation 2021), and in accordance with the State Significant Development Guide prepared 
by DPE, there is a requirement to analyse any feasible alternatives for SSDAs.  
It is considered that the proposal provides for an optimised outcome at the site in lieu of a 
‘Do Nothing’ approach, which would result in the underutilisation of premium industrial 
lands in the Blacktown LGA. 
In addition, it is considered that the proposal represents a better outcome for the site than 
developing the site for the purposes of the service station and the subdivision approved 
under DA-17-01780. In summary, it is considered that the development approved under DA -
17-01780 would have greater  adverse impacts than the proposal  and would not offer the 
social and economic benefits that are offered by the propos al. It is noted that data centres 
have extensive social and economic benefits and are a form of critical infrastructure that are 
vital to the proper functioning of the digital economy. 
In terms of the proposal  itself, multiple design options have been explored. The option 
proposed has been selected as it would result in the most optimal outcome when balancing 
consideration of those matters required to be considered under 4.15(1) of the EP&A Act 1979. 
The proposal provides for a development outcome which will result in the following:  
• The provision of critical infrastructure that will support the economy and community 
alike, resulting in both economic and social benefits; 
• Results in a development outcome which will have minimal adverse built form 
impacts and be compatible with the site and surrounds; 
• Provides a low intensity light industrial land use, which interfaces appropriately with 
the surrounding land and transport network; and

• Ensures that environmental conservation of a portion of the site will continue in

STRATEGIC CONTEXT 
The proposal is located within the Central City District and the Blacktown LGA. A review of 
the District Plan and Blacktown Local Strategic Planning Statement (LSPS)  identifies that  
the proposal is aligned with the respective strategic policies as detailed below.  
The Central City District Plan 
The District Plan identifies the site south of the Blacktown Strategic Centre where it is part 
of a corridor of land between Arndell Park and Pemulwuy. The plan highlights the 
importance of industrial activity and urban services to Greater Sydney’s economy, and the 
nature in which industrial lands are evolving into complex employment lands as a result of 
emerging technologies. The proposal directly supports the realisation of Planning Priority C11 
which is “Maximising opportunities to attract advanced manuf acturing and innovation in 
industrial and urban services land”.  
Blacktown LSPS 2020 
The Blacktown Local Strategic Planning Statement ( LSPS) is Council’s primary strategic 
planning document and sets out planning priorities consistent with the Central City District 
Plan and the Greater Sydney Region Plan, providing a 20 -year land use vision for the LGA. It 
directs how future growth and change will be managed across the LGA and informs changes 
to relevant environmental planning instruments and development control plans.  
The Western Sydney Employment Area (WSEA) is identified in the LSPS as a key strategic 
area for future employment growth, containing approximately 2,500ha of employment 
lands, of which around half is located within the Blacktown LGA. Accordingly, industrial lands 
within WSEA represent a significant proportion of future employment growth opportunity 
for the Blacktown LGA. 
The proposed development supports the delivery of Local Planning Priority 9 as it provides a 
type of ‘high technology industry’ on the site, maximising the use of industrial land that is 
currently undeveloped. It supports Council’s vision for industrial land in the LSPS by 
providing employment opportunities for local workers, and by attracting innovation in the 
form of a high technology industry to the LGA.

EP&A Act 1979 
Section 4.36(2) of the EP&A Act 1979 states that a SEPP may declare any development as 
State Significant Development (SSD). As such, the development constitutes SSD as identified 
under Schedule 1 of SEPP (Planning Systems) 2021. It is noted that the Minister is the consent 
authority for SSD , however, they may delegate this function to staff at the Department of 
Planning and Environment (DPE).  In other circumstances, the Independent Planning 
Commission may also be the Consent Authority. 
The EIS provides an assessment of the proposal against the matters of consideration listed 
in Section 4.15 of the EP&A Act 1979. The assessment undertaken provides that the site is 
suitable for the proposal and within the public interest. 
Furthermore, the EIS outlines the mechanisms available to the Consent Authority for the 
surrendering of consent DA-17-01780. 
EP&A Regulation 2021 
This EIS has been prepared in accordance with the EP&A Regulation 2021 as relevant, with all 
necessary requirements being provided as required under Sections 24 and 190. 
The development would not constitute Designated Development as defined under Schedule 
3 of the Regulation. 
POEO Act 1997 
Schedule 1, Clause 17 (2) identifies that general electricity works  with a capacity to generate 
more than 30 MW of electrical power  constitutes a Scheduled Activity under the POEO Act 
1997. However, it is also noted that Clause 17(1A) under the Schedule identifies that this clause 
does not apply to the generation of electricity by means of electricity plant that is emergency 
stand-by plant operating for less than 200 hours per year. 
Taking into consideration the above, the proposal does  not constitute a Scheduled Activity  
as the testing and use of generators at the site is not anticipated to exceed 200  hours per 
year. 
BC Act 2016 
Under Section 7.9 of the BC Act 2016, a Biodiversity Development Assessment Report (BDAR) 
is required to be submitted for any SSD. 
The BDAR prepared provides an assessment of the impacts associated with the clearing of 
0.34ha of land required to facilitate the proposal.  This includes 0.05ha of Cumberland River 
Flat Forest and 0.29ha of Cumberland Shale Plains Woodland. Both Plant Community Types 
(PCTs) identified within the clearing area have been assessed as conforming to a threatened 
ecological community (TEC) listing. 
The BDAR indicates that the clearing would result in a requirement to retire 8 ecosystem 
credits as calculated in accordance with biodiversity offset scheme established under the BC 
Act 2016.

ASSESSMENT OF IMPACTS 
The EIS addresses  a range of environmental matters identified  in the project’s SEARs. The 
assessment of relevant impacts is summarised below. 
Built Form, urban design and landscaping 
Urban design principles have been adopted to mitigate visual bulk and scale of the proposal, 
when viewed from the public realm and the residential community to the north -east. 
The site master plan and design have been prepared in response to an urban design study 
which considers the site’s context, an in- depth site analysis , and informed by an 
understanding of the desired future character of  the Huntingwood Precinct and its 
realisation as an employment precinct. Furthermore, the design of the proposal has also 
been informed by feedback received from Blacktown City Council received during the Pre-
lodgement process. 
In relation to landscaping, w hilst the operation of the data centre requires the provision of 
visual access and clear sight lines, it is recognised that the proposal also needs to incorporate 
a unified landscape treatment to contribute towards biodiversity, soften built form as viewed 
from the public domain, and enhance visual amenity. This will be achieved through retained 
vegetation and landscape areas. Planted areas will incorporate a mix of medium and large 
canopy trees and an understorey of low shrubs and grasses/groundcovers. Decorative foliage 
and flowering species are to highlight the entry from Reservoir Road as well as access points 
to the building. Tree planting is also proposed within the RMS Road Reserve adjoining the 
eastern boundary of the site adjacent to Reservoir Road.

4.5 Protection of the Environment Operations Act 1997  
Schedule 1 of the Protection of the Environment Operations Act 1997 ( POEO Act 1997 ) lists 
‘scheduled activities’ for which an environment protection license is required.  Clause 17 of 
Schedule 1 lists general electricity works as a scheduled activity, being the generation of 
electricity by means of electricity plant other than wind or solar power.  
Pursuant to Clause 17(A)  of the POEO Act 1997, the generation of electricity by means of 
electricity plant that is emergency stand-by plant operating for less than 200 hours per year 
is not considered a scheduled activity. 
Periodic testing of back -up generators is proposed to take place across the year  at the 
subject site, which as outlined in Section 3.2.8 of this EIS, is proposed to be in the order of 184 
hours per year. Outside of periodic testing, it is unlikely that emergency generators would be 
required to operate for more than 200 hours per annum in a power outage.  
As such, the general electricity works associated with the testing and potential running of 
generators does not trigger the need for an environment pro tection license pursuant to the 
POEO Act 1997.

TRAFFIC GENERATING DEVELOPMENT 
Clause 2.122 of the T&I SEPP 2021  requires development of a certain size or capacity  to be 
referred to Transport for NSW (TfNSW) and for the consent authority to give consideration to 
any submission that is made, the accessibility of the site concerned, and any potential traffic 
safety, road congestion, or parking implications of the development. 
The triggers for referral are contained within Schedule 3 of the T&I SEPP 2021 . Whilst data 
centres are not a specified use, the triggers for any purpose not specified are:  
• 200 or more motor vehicles per hour for sites adjoining any road; and  
• 50 or more motor vehicles per hour for sites with access to a classified road, or within 
90m of a classified road. 
The traffic impact assessment prepared by TPP contained within Appendix 9 of the EIS  
identifies that around 72 car trips would be generated by the development in each peak 
period. Given both Reservoir Road and The Great Western Highway are both classified roads, 
the proposed development is considered traffic generating development in accordance with 
the T&I SEPP 2021. 
4.6.5 State Environmental Planning Policy (Resilience and Hazards) 2021 
CHAPTER 3 – HAZARDOUS AND OFFENSIVE DEVELOPMENT 
State Environmental Planning Policy (Resilience and Hazards) 2021 (R&H SEPP 2021) 
provides criteria to confirm whether a development can be considered potentially hazardous 
or offensive, and outlines how to reduce these impacts. 
A preliminary risk screening assessment undertaken by ACOR to determine the applicability 
of the R&H SEPP 2021 is provided as Appendix 19 and summarised in Section 6.5 of this EIS.  
The risk screening assessment confirms that there are no dangerous goods on the site that 
are included in the threshold values contained within the former SEPP33 and the proposed 
development is therefore not a potentially hazardous industry. As such, no Preliminary 
Hazard Analysis is required to be conducted. 
CHAPTER 4 – REMEDIATION OF LAND 
Chapter 4 of SEPP (R&H) 2021 provides a s tate-wide approach to the remediation of 
contaminated land, in order to reduce the risk of harm to human health or the environment. 
Clause 4.6 of the SEPP requires that a consent authority not grant approval for the carrying 
out of development on land unless: 
a) It has considered whether the land is contaminated; 
b) If the land is contaminated, it is satisfied that it is suitable for the proposed 
development; and 
c) If remediation is required to be made suitable for the proposed development, that 
the land will be remediated before the land is used for that purpose.

provided in Appendix 20 of the EIS. As detailed further in 6.8, the site can be made suitable

managed in accordance with the recommendations of the DSI. 
Taking into consideration the above, it is considered that clause 4.6 of SEPP (R&H) 2021 has

6.6.2 Operational phase impacts  
MODELLING SCENARIOS 
Operational-phase air quality impacts have been assessed using an appropriate and 
approved computational dispersion modelling methodology, performed in accordance with 
the current guidance published by NSW Environment Protection Authority. The assessment 
has considered potential emissions associated with the justified worst -case emissions of all 
emergency standby generators operating at 100 percent load in the event of catastrophic 
power supply failure, and also two realistic scenarios associated with the p lanned and 
controlled testing of the generators on a quarterly and annual testing schedule.  
The impacts of operational phase activities tested under a worst -case scenario (Scenario 1) 
and the two realistic operational scenarios (Scenario 2 and Scenario 3) are discussed below. 
• Scenario 1 – justified worst case scenario - Emergency operation with 48 generators 
(operating at 100% load). 
o This is an incredibly unlikely scenario which would result from catastrophic failure 
in the electricity supply system. However, given that the application includes 48 
generators, this AQIA has assessed the potential impact of those generators 
operating concurrently. 
o All 48 generators have been modelled as operating for all 8,760 hours of the year. 
Should such a catastrophic failure in the electricity supply system occur, this is 
likely to be for a period of 10 to 15 minutes, and therefore the modelling presents 
a conservative assessment of the potential impacts. 
o Given that the likely up-time of the generators would be short-term in nature, only 
assessment of impacts against short -term criteria has been performed, and no 
assessment against annual average criteria is presented, as the results would be 
essentially meaningless. 
o This AQIA provides context around how likely any exceedances of air quality 
criteria would be, given the likelihood of such catastrophic failure.  
• Scenario 2 - realistic operations (quarterly testing) – One (1) generator operating at 
70 % load for 3 0 minutes in each hour, representing the generator testing regime 
performed quarterly. 
o This is a scenario which would regularly occur. One 3  MW generator located on 
the north eastern side of the proposal has been modelled to be tested at any one 
time, between the hours of 7 am and 6 pm, for a period of 30 minutes in each hour. 
Given the high frequency of winds anticipated to occur from south- western 
vectors, the location of the modelled generator was chosen as increased impacts 
would be expected at receptor locations northeast of the p roposal site. This 
provides a level of conservatism to the assessment. 
[[page 92]]
 
90 
 
o Comparison of impacts against >1 hour criteria are performed, providing another 
level of conservatism, as the testing schedule is modelled as occurring for every 
one of the hours between 7 am and 6 pm, on each and every day of the year. 
Testing under this scenario is expected to occur three times a year and 
correspondingly, only 1 38 testing events (4 6 generators × 3 tests) would be 
required each year and the modelling has assumed 4 015 events (11 hours testing 
available per day × 365 days). Modelling is required to be performed in this manner 
to account for all potential combinations of emissions and meteorology.  
o It is noted that stack parameters for the MTU 20V4000 generators are not 
available at 70 % load and correspondingly, generators under Scenario 2 have 
been modelled at 100 % load, which is considered to be conservative.  
• Scenario 3 –  realistic operation (annual testing)  – One (1) generator operating at 
100% load for 65 minutes in sequence, representing the generator testing regime 
performed annually. 
o This scenario is expected to occur once a year. The same 3 MW generator location 
modelled under Scenario 2 has been used to model Scenario 3, given the level of 
conservatism, modelled as occurring between 7 am and 6 pm every day of the 
year to account for variations of potential combinations of emissions and 
meteorology. 
o Similarly, predicted impacts against >1-hour criteria are performed. 
o Testing under Scenario 3 is anticipated to result in only 48 testing events required 
each year while modelling has also assumed 4 015 events providing a significant 
level of conservatism for this assessment.

Risks posed would be addressed through the development of an environmental

Built heritage measures

Archaeological measures 
Unexpected finds protocol  – If unexpected finds of historical nature are discovered during 
any work, work within 5m of the find must cease and the following steps taken:  
• An archaeologist will be contacted to assess the find and determine if it is clearly a 
relic or has moderate to high potential to be a relic (this may require additional 
research); 
• If the find is determined to be a relic, a s146 (of the Heritage Act) is to be forwarded to 
the Heritage Council who will be consulted on the appropriate management 
measure; and 
• If the find is assessed and is not a relic, work inside the area that was made a no -go 
area can re-commence. 
Human remains protocol – In the event that known or suspected human remains (generally 
in skeletal form) are encountered during the activity, the following procedure will be followed 
immediately upon discovery: 
• All work in the immediate vicinity will cease and the find will be immediately reported

senior staff member; 
• The Environment Manager or other nominated senior staff member will promptly 
notify the police (as required for all human remains discoveries);  
• The Environment Manager or other nominated senior staff member will contact 
Heritage NSW for advice on identification of the human remains;  
• If it is determined that the human remains are Aboriginal ancestral remains, the Local 
Aboriginal Land Council will be contacted, and consultative arrangements will be 
made to discuss ongoing care of the remains; and 
• If it is determined that the human remains are not Aboriginal ancestral remains, the 
coroner will be contacted by the police and further investigation will be conducted to 
determine if the remains represent a historical grave or if police involvement is 
required.

6.19 Crime Prevention 
Crime Prevention through Environmental Design (CPTED) is a crime prevention strategy 
which reduces opportunities for crime by using design and place management principles.  
Patch has relied on the Crime prevention and the assessment of development applications: 
Guidelines under section 79C of the Environmental Planning and Assessment Act 1979 
guidelines prepared by the NSW Department of Urban Affairs and Planning (2001). The 
guidelines can be applied in the assessment of development applications to ensure that 
approvals are not creating or exacerbating crime risk. 
Four principles are included in the guidelines that need to be used in the assessment of 
applications to minimise the opportunity for crime: 
• Surveillance; 
• Access control; 
• Territorial reinforcement; and 
• Space management. 
Surveillance 
Good surveillance is provided when people can see what others are doing. The layout of the 
proposal ensures sightlines into and out of the site are maintained, with open fencing 
proposed, and carparking areas open and well surveyed.  
Access Control 
The site will be security monitored, with access controlled via boom gates, accessible via 
swipe card. Visitors and delivery drivers will have access provided via security at the 
gatehouse.  
Territorial Reinforcement 
Territorial reinforcement relates to community ownership of public spaces, and can be 
achieved through design that encourages gathering, provides clear transitions and 
boundaries between public and private spaces, and includes design cues on who is to use 
spaces and for what purposes.  
The principle of territorial reinforcement is not strictly relevant to the proposal given that it is 
not a public space. Nevertheless, territorial reinforcement will be provided by strong barrier 
fencing and landscaping which will indicate the land as private property.  
Space Management 
Space management strategies include site cleanliness, rapid repair of vandalism and graffiti, 
the replacement of burned -out pedestrian and car park lighting and the removal or 
refurbishment of decayed physical elements.  These are ongoing matters which will be 
implemented into operations.

6.21 Social and Economic Impacts 
Ethos Urban has been engaged to undertake Social and Economic Impact Assessment 
(SEIA) in relation to the proposal which is provided as Appendix 30 of the EIS.  
The SEIA finds that the proposal  will deliver a development outcome  within a strategic 
employment precinct that will encourage growth of higher order jobs within Western 
Sydney. Specifically, the proposal  will accommodate up the 250 direct FTE jobs during the 
construction phase, and 74 direct FTE jobs once completed and fully operational. 
More importantly, the  proposal will result in the provision of critical infrastructure that is 
increasingly important within the emerging digital  economy and represent s a significant 
private investment in the NSW. The proposal is a direct response to demand within the NSW 
market for such facilities and will deliver infrastructure that provides a secure, reliable, and 
cost-effective way to store and process data, while also offering scalability and high levels of 
uptime. Specifically, it will support the following trends driving increasing demand for such 
facilities as outlined in the SEIA: 
• Businesses and governments are increasingly outsourcing data storage , driven 
by demand to improve efficiency, meeting changing technology requirements, and 
to ensure businesses have the ability to increase scale if need (CBRE 2022). In 
addition to this, the demand for data storage and security by public sector entities is 
increasing, where sectors such as government departments and health providers 
have significant cloud storage requirements and are increasingly concerned about 
public data security. 
• Increased cloud computing  is forecast to support industry growth in data centres 
over the next five years (IBIS World –  Cloud Storage Services in Australia). This 
includes an increase in internet connections and traffic by both business and 
individuals. In fact, in Sydney, some 75 % of demand for data centres is driven by 
demand from cloud computing (JLL, H1 2022). The benefits of cloud computing for 
businesses is becoming increasingly realised, including reduced technology costs 
per employee, expedited info rmation sharing and access to all information from 
various platforms (CBRE, 2022). 
• The rise in e-commerce – In recent years, consumer and business expectations have 
evolved, resulting from improved technology particularly in online store platforms. 
The take up of online shopping in particular has increased demand for online 
infrastructure, placing pressure on retailers to invest in data storage facilities. Online 
retail penetration rates in Australia are forecast to increase from 13.2% to 18% by 2025 
(Colliers, 2021). 
While it is considered that the proposal will have a significantly positive impact, the SEIA 
identified the potential for some negative social impacts to result from the proposal also. The 
predominant negative social impacts identified were associated with a further loss  of 
vegetation at the site, air pollution, and contribution to urban heat in Western Sydney. While 
these impacts are acknowledged, it is considered that through the adoption of appropriate 
mitigation measures they can be appropriately managed as detailed throughout this report 
and in the mitigation measures table provided as Appendix 3 of the EIS.
