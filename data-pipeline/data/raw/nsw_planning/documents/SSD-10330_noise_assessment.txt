1 Introduction 
SLR Consulting Australia Pty Ltd (SLR) has been engaged by Canberra Data Centres Pty Ltd (CDC) to prepare a 
noise impact assessment (NIA ) to accompany the State Significant D evelopment Application (SSDA)  for a 
proposed Data Centre at 17 Roberts Road, Eastern Creek (the Project).  The site is formally described as Lot 2 
DP 1159804, and has an approximate area of 14.52 hectares.   
An existing Data Centre with associated office buildings and plant is located to the east of the site.  This facility 
has been acquired and will form part of the combined facility operated by CDC.  Hence this noise assessment 
considers the cumulative noise impact of the operation of the existing and the proposed data centres. 
The Secretary's Environmental Assessment Requirements (SEARs) for the Project were received on 7 July 2019.  
The SEARs states that, as part of the EIS assessment, the following noise and vibration matters must be 
addressed: 
A quantitative noise and vibration assessment undertaken by a suitably qualified person in accordance with the 
relevant Environment Protection Authority guidelines and including an assessment of nearby sensitive 
receivers. 
Cumulative impacts of other developments. 
Details of proposed mitigation, management and monitoring measures. 
Accordingly this assessment has been undertaken to address the above requirements for construction and 
operational noise and vibration  with reference to the following NSW Environment Protection Authority (EPA) 
guidelines: 
 NSW Noise Policy for Industry (NPfI) (EPA, 2017). 
 Interim Construction Noise Guideline (ICNG) (EPA, 2009). 
 Assessing Vibration: a technical guideline (EPA, 2006)

3.2 Meteorological Environment 
3.2.1 Wind 
Wind has the potential to increase noise at a receiver when it is light and stable and blows from the di rection 
of the source of the noise.  As the strength of the wind increases , the noise produced by the wind will obscure 
noise from most industrial and transport sources. 
Wind effects need to be considered when wind is a feature of the area under considera tion (in accordance 
with the NPfI).  Where wind blows from the source to the receiver at speeds up to 3 m/s for more than 30% of 
the time in any season, then wind is considered to be a feature of the area and noise level predictions must be 
made under these conditions. 
Wind speed and direction data, among other parameters, ha s been obtained from the BOM automated 
weather station (AWS) at BOM station (Horsley Park Equestrian Centre [station number 67119]), 
approximately 4 km south -east of the Development site.  Consistent with the results of the SLR Air Quality 
Impact Assessment (Report 610.18883-R03) prevailing winds are a feature of the area.  To represent enhanced 
noise propagation, a 3 m/s wind prevailing from the south west has been assessed. 
3.2.2 Temperature Inversion 
Temperature inversions, when they occur, have the ability to increase noise levels by focusing sound waves.  
Temperature inversions occur predominantly at night during the winter months.  For a temperature inversion 
to be a significant characteri stic of the area it needs to occur for approximately 30% of the total night -time 
during winter, or about two nights per week.   
Temperature inversions occur during E, F and G stability categories. These three categories are considered to 
represent weak, moderate and strong inversions respectively.  For noise-assessment purposes, only moderate 
and strong inversions are considered significant enough to require assessment

4.2.2 Project Amenity and Project Intrusiveness Noise Levels 
Noise objectives for the assessment of residential and industrial/commercial facility  receivers are detailed in 
the NPfI , as administered by the EPA.  In implementing the NPfI, the EPA has two broad objectives: 
 Controlling intrusive noise levels in the short term; and

 Maintaining noise amenity levels for particular land uses over the medium to long-term. 
In general terms, the NPfI sets out procedures fo r establishing the project intrusiveness L Aeq(15minute) and 
project amenity LAeq(period) noise levels, with a view of determining the lower (that is, the more stringent) being 
the Project Trigger Noise Level (PTNL), NPfI Section 2.1 states: 
The project int rusiveness noise level aims to protect against significant changes in noise levels, whilst 
the project amenity noise level seeks to protect against cumulative noise impacts from industry and 
maintain amenity for particular land uses.  Applying the most str ingent requirement as the project 
noise trigger level ensures that both intrusive noise is limited and amenity is protected and that no 
single industry can unacceptably change the noise level of an area. 
Implementation is achieved by ensuring that: 
 Noise from any single source does not intrude greatly above the prevailing background noise level.  
This is known as the intrusive noise criterion; and 
 The background noise level does not exceed the level appropriate for the particular locality and land 
use.  This is known as the amenity criterion. 
In order to satisfy the above requirements, an intrusive and amenity noise criterion is determined, of which 
the lower is usually adopted as the Project Noise Trigger Levels (PTNLs).

As outlined in Section 2.2 to maintain uninterrupted power to the Data Centre during an emergency power

Periodic testing of the individual generators will occur during the daytime or evening periods and would occur

5 Vibration Assessment Criteria 
5.1 Categories of Vibration in Structures 
The effects of vibration in buildings can be divided into three main categories; those in which the occupants or 
users of the building are inconvenienced or possibly disturbed, those where the building contents may be 
affected and those in which the integrity of the building or the structure itself may be compromised. 
For the Facility construction works, the nearest structures are the commercial buildings adjacent to the north, 
and adjacent to the south.  The nearest residential dwellings are located on Denison  Street and approximately 
150 m to the east. 
5.2 Assessment Criteria 
Most commonly specified ‘safe’ structural vibration limits are designed to minimise the risk of cosmetic 
damage such as surface cracks, and are set well below the levels that have potential t o cause structural 
damage.  The British Standard BS 7385 Part 2 -1993 Evaluation and measurement for vibration in buildings 
Part 2, provides frequency-dependent vibration limits related to the cosmetic damage risk.   
The German Standard DIN 4150-3:1999 “Structural Vibration Part 3: Effects of vibration in structures” provides 
guideline values for evaluating the effect of vibration on sensitive structures, such as buried pipework.  In 
addition, SLR has identified appropriate vibration criteria for the operati ng of a mechanical plant where 
applicable. 
The EPA’s “ Assessing Vibration: A Technical Guideline”  (DEC 2006) provides guideline building vibration levels 
associated with a low probability of annoyance from occupants.  The applicable damage risk and annoyan ce

8 CONCLUSION 
SLR Consulting Australia Pty Ltd (SLR) has been engaged by Canberra Data Centres Pty Ltd (CDC) to prep are an 
Noise Impact Assessment to accompany the SSD application for the proposed datacentre at  17 Roberts Road, 
Eastern Creek.   
An existing Data Centre with associated office building and plant is located to the east of the site.  This facility 
has been acquired and will form part of the combined facility operated by CDC.  Hence this noise assessm ent 
considers the combined or cumulative noise impact of the operation of the existing and proposed Data 
Centres.  The results of the study are summarised in the following points: 
 An ambient noise surveys was  conducted and project specific operational nois e criteria were 
developed in accordance with the NSW EPA’s Noise Policy for Industry.  Goals were set for nearby 
residents and also set for the nearby commercial receivers.   
 Noise emissions from the operational activities from the combined f acility are predicted to be below 
or to meet the project trigger noise levels at all of the residential noise assessment locations during 
the daytime and evening.  During the night-time under enhanced noise propagating conditions, there 
is a 5 dB exceedance of the PTNL at Flavex Lane receivers.. 
 Conceptual mitigation measures enabling compliance with the night-time project trigger noise levels 
will be further investigated during detailed design phase of the project. The mitigation measures 
would include acoustic screens around the plant decks and would also be required to mitigate noise 
levels to ensure modifying factors detailed in table C1 of the NPfI are not a characteristic of the site.  
 At commercial receivers compliance with the project trigger noise levels is predicted at all locations  
during calm conditions.  During a south westerly wind a minor 2  dB exceedance is predicted at the 
adjoining commercial receiver to the north.  This residual commercial exceedance is considered to be 
negligible.  
 Noise emissions from construction activities are predicted to be below the noise management levels 
of the EPA’s Interim Construction Noise Guideline. 
 Diesel generator operation during testing complies with the EPA’s Noise Policy for Industry .  It is 
recommended the proposed additional diesel generators are specified to have sound power levels no 
higher than the existing diesel generators on site.

the measurement p arameter used to describe 
the road traffic noise level over the entire night -
time (10.00  pm to 7.00  am) period.  Similarly, 
the L Aeq(1hour) is the measurement parameter

A-Weighting or dBA Noise Levels 
The overall level of a sound is usually expressed in 
terms of dBA, which is measured using the “A -
weighting” filter incorporated in sound level meters.  
These filters have a frequency response 
corresponding approximately to that of human 
hearing.  People’s hearing is most sensitive to 
sounds at mid frequencies (500  Hz to 4000  Hz), and 
less sensitive at lower and higher frequencies.  Thus, 
the level of a sound in dBA is a good measure of the 
“loudness” of that sound.  Different sources having 
the same dBA level generally sound about equally as 
loud, although the perceived loudness can also be 
affected by the character of the sound (eg  the 
loudness of human speech and a distant motorbike 
may be perceived differently, although they are of 
the same dBA level). 
Sensitivity of People to Noise Level Changes 
A change of up to 3  dBA in the level of a sound is 
difficult for most people to detect, whilst a 3  dBA to 
5 dBA change corresponds to a small but noticeable 
change in loudness.  A 10  dBA change correspond s 
to an approximate doubling or halving in loudness.

Vibration - Building Structures Cosmetic Damage Risk 
Most commonly  specified ‘safe’ structural vibration limits are designed to minimise the risk of cosmetic 
damage such as surface cracks, and are set well below the levels that have potential to cause structural 
damage.  British Standard BS 7385 Part 2 -1993 Evaluation and measurement for vibration in buildings Part 2,  
provides frequency-dependent vibration limits related to the cosmetic damage risk.  Noting, cosmetic damage 
is very minor in nature, is readily repairable and does not affect the structural integrity of the building.  
The BS 7385 Part 2 -1993 sets guide values for building vibration based on the lowest vibration levels above 
which damage has been credibly demonstrated.  These levels are judged to give a minimum risk of 
vibration-induced damage, where minimal r isk for a named effect is usually taken as a 95% probability of no 
effect.  Sources of vibration that are considered in the standard include demolition, blasting (carried out 
during mineral extraction or construction excavation), piling, ground treatments (eg compaction), construction 
equipment, tunnelling, road and rail traffic and industrial machinery. 
The recommended limits (guide values) for transient vibration to ensure minimal risk of cosmetic damage to 
residential and industrial buildings are presented numerically in Table E1 and graphically in Figure E1.

component particle velocity.  This is not inconsistent with an extensive review of the case history 
information available in the UK.” 
Also that: 
“A building of historical value should not (u nless it is structurally unsound) be assumed to be more 
sensitive.” 
BS 7385 Part 2-1993 states that the guide values in Table E1 relate predominantly to transient vibration which 
does not give rise to resonant responses in structures and low -rise buildings.  Where the dynamic loading

resonance, especially at the lower frequencies where lower guide values apply, then the guide values in 
Table E1 may need to be reduced by up to 50%.
