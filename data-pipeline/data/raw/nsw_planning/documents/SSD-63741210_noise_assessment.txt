Executive summary 
This Noise and Vibration assessment has been prepared by Aurecon on behalf of NEXTDC Limited to 
accompany a detailed State Significant Development Application (SSDA) for the proposed S4 data centre 
development at 16 Johnston Crescent, Horsley Park. The site is legally described as Lot 305 in Deposited 
Plan 1275011.  
This report has been prepared to address the Secretary’s Environmental Assessment Requirements 
(SEARs) issued for the project (SSD-63741210). The assessment considers the construction and 
operational phases of the development including the noise from maintenance testing of back-up power 
systems.  
The noise and vibration assessment undertaken for the project involved the following steps:  
◼ Measurement of existing background and ambient noise levels at locations representative of the sensitive 
receivers in accordance with the NSW EPA Noise Policy for Industry (NPfI). 
◼ Deriving project specific operational and construction noise criteria based on measured noise levels  and 
applicable NSW policies. 
◼ Developing a construction and operational equipment itinerary and predicting the construction and 
operational phase noise levels at the nearest sensitive receivers using computational noise modelling.  
◼ Comparing the modelled noise impacts with the stipulated noise criteria specific at sensitive receivers. 
◼ Determining the effect of potential vibration impacts on buildings and structures  during construction. 
◼ Developing feasible mitigation measures to achieve compliance. 
◼ Addressing residual noise, if applicable.  
 
The report concludes that the proposed data centre development is suitable and warrants approval subject 
to the implementation of mitigation measures summarised in the discussions below and described in detail in 
Section 7: 
Construction noise and vibration: 
The project includes the construction of five datacentre buildings together with office facilities and associated 
landscaping and civil works. Construction of the various buildings on the site is likely to be staged, with 
construction of the buildings being delivered over a number of years. At this stage, the detailed construction 
program of the various buildings has not been finalised. Criteria for construction impacts have been 
established in accordance with the relevant guidelines.  
Recommendations with regard to mitigation and management strategies of construction noise and vibration 
impacts have been outlined in Sections 7.1. Safe working distances have been provided to reduce the 
vibration impacts at receivers. However, due to the location of the nearest receivers, these may not be 
achievable in all cases. Vibration monitoring during construction activities and less vibration intensive 
methods, such as using bored piling techniques rather than impact piling at locations closest to the 
residential properties may be required in the construction process. 
Once further details of the construction are confirmed, a detailed construction noise and vibration 
management plan will need to be compiled and properly communicated with affected residents, prior to 
commencement of works. Agreements with residents for acceptable work periods and periods of respite are 
also recommended in the relevant noise guideline documents for New South Wales.  
Construction traffic noise: 
It is anticipated that deliveries to site during construction will be via Old Wallgrove road and Johnston 
Crescent which is directed away from the nearest residential receivers. No construction traffic is expected on 
Burley Road directly adjacent the residences. 
[[page 5]]
 
  
Detailed construction traffic data is not currently available for the site and therefore has not been assessed. 
Further review of the expected increase in road traffic during construction is required, however, it is 
anticipated that only a few deliveries will occur over the course of the day and as such, the likelihood of a 
significant noise impact from construction traffic is low. If significant delivery activities are expected, these 
should be managed and properly communicated with the residents to reduce the likelihood of disturbance.  
Operational noise: 
A computational noise model has been constructed for the site based on the current design and assumptions 
for operational noise scenarios.   
With the implementation of feasible and reasonable noise mitigation measures, noise modelling indicates 
that the project trigger levels will not be exceeded at the nearest receivers. 
Mitigation measures for operational noise include the following and are discussed in detail in Section 7.2: 
◼ Custom designed substation transformers (to achieve a sound power level of 84dBA or less) surrounded 
by fire/noise walls with internal sound absorbing finish. 
◼ Cooling towers with quieter fans. Sound absorbing screens will also be used to block line of sight to the 
nearest receivers. 
◼ Attenuators at louvres to: 
− Chiller plant 
− CRAH intakes 
− FWU intakes 
◼ Custom acoustic generator enclosures 
◼ Internal sound absorbing lining to exhaust chimneys from data halls. 
◼ Quieter OAP units for Buildings B to E 
◼ Avoiding heavy vehicle movements within the site during the night time period  
◼ Locating car parking spaces such that they do not have a direct line of sight to the eastern residential 
receivers. 
Cumulative noise 
Following a review of the relevant nearby developments either in construction or approved for construction at 
the time of our assessment, the cumulative noise contribution from NEXTDC S4 Data Centre and all 
currently approved future projects near the Project site is estimated to be equal to or below the 
Recommended Amenity Levels for the sensitive receivers. Further discussion is provided in Section 6.3.

for the proposed NEXTDC S4 data centre development at 16 Johnston Crescent, Horsley Park (SSD-
63741210).

4 Methodology 
4.1 Noise Assessment approach  
A quantitative noise assessment has been undertaken for the project which involved the following steps: 
◼ Determining the baseline conditions for background and ambient noise levels at the sensitive receivers in 
accordance with EPA NSW Noise Policy for Industry (NPfI.) and Interim Construction Noise Guideline 
(ICNG). 
◼ Deriving the relevant criteria for the project: 
− Deriving the project specific operational noise criteria based on measured background noise levels 
and the amenity noise levels. 
− Deriving the project specific construction noise criteria depending on the type of the sensitive receiver 
and considering the measured background noise levels. 
− Identifying the project specific construction vibration criteria. 
◼ Identifying construction and operational equipment itinerary 
◼ Estimating the noise impacts at the nearest sensitive receivers using computational 3D noise modelling.  
◼ Estimating vibration impacts at the nearest sensitive receivers using empirical formulae. 
◼ Comparison of the predicted impacts with the stipulated noise / vibration criteria. 
◼ Developing feasible and reasonable mitigation measures to ameliorate the impacts in case of 
exceedances. 
4.2 Study area 
The study area was defined by examining the site context, the sensitive receivers and the surrounding area. 
An area spanning an approximately 1km radius from the site boundary was chosen. The nearest residences 
were identified as the relevant noise sensitive receivers, as outlined in Section 4.2.2, in addition to 
identification of NCAs. Compliance of noise at these receivers indicates a compliance at receivers located 
further from the site.  
Figure 4-1 illustrates the project study area and the surrounding land-use zoning in accordance with the 
Fairfield Local Environment Plan 2013.

4.3 Computational noise model 
A computational noise model was developed for the site to predict environmental noise emissions from the 
Project during its construction and operational phases. Noise emissions for the Project were calculated using 
the CONCAWE noise propagation model within SoundPLAN noise software (version 9.0). CONCAWE is a 
noise propagation model that has been widely used for calculating the level of noise radiated into 
surrounding communities by most industrial sound sources. 
The CONCAWE model incorporates the following with the calculations performed in 1/1 octave frequency 
bands between 63 Hz and 8000 Hz: 
◼ Noise attenuation over distance 
◼ Atmospheric absorption 
◼ Ground effects

4.3.3 Limitations and uncertainty  
The following are the main limitations for the noise modelling: 
◼ It has been assumed that all equipment is operating continuously and simultaneously over the 
assessment period during both construction and operation of the project providing the most conservative 
noise predictions.  
◼ At the time of the assessment, the preferred equipment supplier has not been confirmed and therefore a

absence of an appointed supplier, this noise and vibration assessment is based on the Project concept 
design and a range of supplier data. Upon appointment of the successful supplier, confirmation should be 
provided that the selected equipment is able to perform within the predictions of this assessment. An 
acoustic review is required during the detailed design stage and also post construction and should 
consider the presence of any modifying factors for the noise in accordance with the NPfI. Where 
exceedances are observed, mitigation measures and management controls should be implemented to 
bring the noise and vibration to within acceptable limits. 
◼ The assessment is based on the assumptions in Section 4.3.1. 
◼ It has been presumed that data such as building heights from Open Street Maps or the elevation contours 
from ELVIS (Elevation and Depth - Foundation Spatial Data) are accurate and current at the time of the 
assessment.  
◼ Equipment for a fire emergency has not been modelled. 
◼ Audible noise from power lines has not been modelled and considered negligible compared to the other 
noise sources. 
4.4 Vibration Assessment Methodology 
Construction work has the potential to generate vibration which can impact human comfort and/or cause 
structural damage to buildings.  
Vibration is generated when energy from equipment is transmitted into the ground and is generally attenuated 
with distance. Vibration level of a source is inversely proportional to the distance between the source and 
receiver. The magnitude and attenuation of ground vibration is dependent on the following:  
◼ the ground type and topography  
◼ the impact medium stiffness  
◼ the efficiency of the energy transfer mechanism of the equipment (ie impulsive, reciprocating, rolling or 
rotating equipment)  
◼ the frequency content, and 
◼ the type of wave (surface or body)  
Due to the above factors, there is inherent variability in ground vibration predictions without site -specific 
measurement data. To obtain an estimate, existing research data has been used to predict the vibration levels 
during certain vibration intensive tasks.

5.1.1 Vibration 
The effect of vibration impacts on buildings and structures can be divided into two categories:   
◼ Human comfort impacts – where the occupants or users of the affected building are possibly disturbed by 
the construction vibration  
◼ Structural impacts – effects on building contents and structural integrity  
The vibration levels at which people become annoyed are well below vibration levels at which structural 
damage occurs.

Project intrusiveness noise level 
The intrusiveness noise level seeks to limit the degree of change a new noise source introduces to an 
existing environment. A noise source would generally be considered non -intrusive if the monitored average 
noise level (LAeq) for a period does not exceed the RBL by more than 5 dB(A). Intrusive noise levels are only 
applied to residential receivers (residences).  
LAeq,15min=rating background noise level+5 dB 
Project amenity noise level 
To limit continuing increases in noise levels from application of the intrusiveness level alone, the ambient 
noise level within an area from all industrial noise sources combined should remain below the recommended 
amenity noise levels specified in Table 6-1 where feasible and reasonable. The recommended amenity noise 
levels will protect against noise impacts such as speech interference, community annoyance and some sleep 
disturbance. 
Since the night-time amenity noise criterion is the most stringent, it is the controlling time -period. To account 
for converting LAeq, period to LAeq,15min, the NPfI accepts a default conversion factor of LAeq,15min =LAeq, period + 3 dB.

Project Amenity noise level LAeq,15min= recommended amenity noise level - 5 dB +3 dB

Corrections for annoying noise characteristics 
Table C1 of the NPfI provides corrections for tonality, intermittency, irregularity, or dominant low -frequency 
content. It should be noted that in accordance with the NPfI, it is not important in the assessment of 
modifying factors if the actual individual noise sources contain tonal, intermittent, or low frequency noise 
characteristics, but whether the resulting noise impact (as measured or assessed at the sensitive receivers) 
contains tonal, intermittent, or low frequency noise characteristics. 
The corrections specified for tonal, intermittent, and low-frequency noise are to be added to the measured or 
predicted noise levels at the receiver before comparison with the project noise trigger levels. The 
adjustments for duration are to be applied to the criterion.  
We note that, while the back-up power generators would usually be termed as a one-off event, in the case of 
a data centre, they can operate for more than 2.5 hours. Hence the duration correction doesn’t apply for the 
back-up power testing scenario. 
Low frequency noise correction 
A difference of 15 dB or more between the C- and A-weighted noise measurements, identifies the potential 
for an unbalanced spectrum and an increased likelihood of low frequency noise annoyance.  
The difference between C- and A-weighted noise levels is typically used as a screening tool to determine if 
further investigation is required to determine potential annoyance (ie where the difference is 15 dB or more). 
Where further investigation confirms significant low frequency content, a low frequency noise correction is 
applied to the predicted or measured noise levels.   
The NPfI identifies that the corrections should “reflect external assessment locations”, or sensitive receiver 
locations so the existing noise environment should be considered.

6.2.2 Operating Scenarios 
Noise sources in these scenarios are from equipment associated with data centre operation, back-up power 
and the substation transformers. Although the mechanical plant is likely to operate at a reduced capacity 
during certain periods, a conservative approach has been taken by assuming 100% operation during all the 
assessment periods. 
Operational noise has been modelled as follows for the day, evening & night time assessment periods of the 
NPfI: 
Stage 1: operation of Buildings A, B, C and the substation equipment continuously and simultaneously at 
100 % capacity over a 15-minute period during each assessment period under standard and noise-
enhancing meteorological conditions. 
Stage 2: operation of buildings i.e. A, B, C and D and the substation equipment continuously and 
simultaneously at 100 % capacity over a 15-minute period during each assessment period under standard 
and noise-enhancing meteorological conditions. 
Stage 3: operation of all buildings i.e. A, B, C ,D ,E and the substation equipment continuously and 
simultaneously at 100 % capacity over a 15-minute period during each assessment period under standard  
and noise-enhancing meteorological conditions. 
Note, emergency scenario is where there is a complete power failure for the data centre and all back up 
generators are operational.  
We understand that the substation transformers could be operating in one of two possible configurations:

2. Fail / N-1 operation: 3x 150MVA transformers operational at 150MVA load, with the 4th transformer 
de-energised (offline) 
Scenario 2 generates a marginally higher noise level and has been assumed in the model.

The predicted noise was analysed at each receiver and modifying factors were determined quantitatively in 
accordance with the NPfI Fact Sheet C.  
◼ Tonality - 1/3rd Octave band data was not available for the equipment noise levels. Hence narrow band 
analysis using the reference method in ISO 1996-2007, Annex C was not possible.  
◼ Low frequency - A difference of 15 dB or more between the C - and A-weighted noise levels, identifies the 
potential for an unbalanced spectrum and an increased likelihood of low frequency noise annoyance. The 
difference between C- and A-weighted noise levels is typically used as a screening tool to determine if 
further investigation is required to determine potential annoyance (ie where the difference is 15 dB or 
more). Where further investigation confirms significant low frequency content, a low frequency noise 
correction is applied to the predicted or measured noise levels.

6.3 Cumulative impacts 
The NPfI defines cumulative industrial noise levels as the total level of noise from all industrial sources. 
Potential effects of cumulative noise must be considered, and reasonable steps must be demonstrated to 
ensure that the combined noise contribution from all commercial, industrial and trade premises does not 
exceed the relevant noise criteria at each of the NCAs per NPfI. Operational noise levels from existing 
projects are captured in the ambient noise survey in Section 4.2.3. The effects of future major projects which 
may contribute to the cumulative noise are considered in this section. 
The NPfI notes that the Project Amenity Noise Level is used to limit noise from the NEXTDC S4 
development. It also states that the ambient noise level within an area from all industrial noise sources 
combined should remain below the Recommended Amenity Noise Levels (RANL). The relevant criteria 
for the residential receivers within the NCAs are detailed in Table 6-1 and also in Table 6-29 (RANL 
LAeq,15minute = RANL per period LAeq, period+ 3dB in accordance with the NPfI).

impacts, are indicated in Figure 6-3. These Projects were identified using the DPHI Major Projects Planning

Assessment Guidelines for State Significant Projects (DPIE, 2022), relevant future projects considered for 
the cumulative impact assessment include: 
◼ Projects which may have intensity of operations change over time (e.g. the project is currently operating

8 Conclusion  
This report has been prepared to address the Secretary’s Environmental Assessment Requirements 
(SEARs) issued for the project (SSD-63741210). The assessment considers the construction and 
operational phases of the development including the noise from the use of back-up power.  
Based on the current design and associated assumptions, noise from operation of the data centre and 
associated cumulative noise impacts are predicted to be equal or below the project trigger level and amenity 
levels provided the recommended mitigation and management strategies are implemented for the site.   
Recommendations for mitigation and management of construction noise and vibration impacts have been 
outlined in Sections 7.1. Once further details of the construction are confirmed, a detailed construction noise 
and vibration management plan will need to be compiled and properly communicated with affected residents, 
prior to commencement of works. The contractor should identify all reasonable and practicable methods to 
reduce construction noise and vibration impacts. This may include using low noise and vibration alternatives 
and developing foundation designs that avoid the use of impact piles. Agreements with residents for 
acceptable work periods and periods of respite are also recommended in the relevant noise guideline 
documents for New South Wales.

Sound pressure at a point is the local pressure deviation from the 
ambient (average or equilibrium) atmospheric pressure, caused by a 
sound wave. In air, sound pressure can be measured using a 
microphone, and in water with a hydrophone. The SI unit of sound 
pressure is the pascal (Pa). 
Sound Pressure Level 
(SPL or Lp) 
Sound pressure level is equal to 10 times the logarithm (to base 10) of 
the ratio of the rms sound pressure squared to a reference sound 
pressure squared. The reference value for sound pressure levels is 20 
µPa.

Temperature inversion A factor that can impact sound propagation over long distances is 
temperature gradients in the atmosphere. On a typical sunny afternoon, 
air is warmest near the ground and temperature decreases at higher 
altitudes. This temperature gradient causes sound waves to refract 
upward, away from the ground and results in lower noise levels being 
heard at the listener’s position. In the evening, this temperature gradient 
will reverse, resulting in cooler temperatures near the ground. This 
condition, often referred to is a temperature inversion will cause sound to 
bend downward toward the ground and results in louder noise levels at 
the listener position. 
Wind gradient Wind alters sound propagation by the mechanism of refraction; that is, 
wind bends sound waves. Wind nearer to the ground moves more slowly 
than wind at higher altitudes, due to surface characteristics such as hills, 
trees, and man-made structures that interfere with the wind. This wind 
gradient, with faster wind at higher elevation and slower wind at lower 
elevation causes sound waves to bend downward when they are 
traveling to a location downwind of the source and to bend upward when 
traveling toward a location upwind of the source. Waves bending 
downward means that a listener standing downwind of the source will 
hear louder noise levels than the listener standing upwind of the source. 
This phenomenon can significantly impact sound propagation over long 
distances and when wind speeds are high.

Table A 4 Glossary of vibration terms 
Term Definition  
Amplitude The measurement of energy or movement in a vibrating object. Amplitude 
is measured and expressed in three ways:  
◼ Displacement (typically in mm Pk-Pk);  
◼ Velocity (typically in mm/s Pk); and  
◼ Acceleration (typically in m/s2 RMS).  
Amplitude is also the y-axis of the vibration time waveform and spectrum; 
it helps define the severity of the vibration. 
Frequency The repetition rate of a periodic vibration, per unit of time, determined by 
taking the reciprocal of the period (T).  Frequency is expressed in: Hz 
(how many cycles per second); Frequency is also the x-axis of the 
vibration spectrum. 
Hertz (Hz) Vibration can occur over a range of frequencies extending from the very 
low, such as the rumble of thunder, up to the very high such as the crash 
of cymbals.  The frequency of vibration and sound is measured in hertz 
(Hz). Once hertz is one cycle per second.  Structural Vibration is 
generally measured over the frequency range from 1Hz to 500Hz 
(0.5kHz). 
Peak to Peak (Pk-Pk) This is the measure of the vibration amplitude, maximum to minimum, 
equal to twice of the RMS value of a sine wave. 
Peak particle velocity 
(PPV or Vi max) 
The maximum resultant particle velocity Vi max characterizes the vibration 
severity 
Peak vector sum The resultant particle velocity magnitude or vector sum of the transverse, 
vertical and longitudinal particle velocity components. 
RMS velocity For most applications where there is continuous vibration, vibration is 
measured in terms of root mean square RMS velocity (mm/s).
