2.0 Existing Noise Environment 
The existing noise environment forms a component of the noise criteria for the project.  To 
quantify and characterise the existing noise environment of the area, noise monitoring was 
conducted near to residential receptors in Firethorne Place (R01) in November 2024. 
The Monitoring Location (ML01) has been shown in Figure 1 and will be representative of 
the potentially most-affected residential receptors near the project.   
The selected monitoring location was adjacent to the nearest residential receptors to the 
project.  At the selected location, the ambient noise levels would likely be the lowest in the 
area for all potentially affected residences.  In selecting the location, the following was 
considered: 
• Exposure to road traffic noise.  The selected monitoring location would be 
representative of the rear and side of the nearest residences to the project with the 
same level of exposure to traffic noise from vehicles on Tompsitt Drive and ‘Jerra 
Circle’. 
Residential locations to the north of Tompsitt Drive are closer to Tompsitt Drive and 
‘Jerra Circle’ and therefore would be likely to be exposed to higher traffic noise levels 
from vehicles on those roads.  Consequently, higher noise limits would be expected 
to apply at those residences. 
• Exposure to construction and retail/commercial noise.  Residential locations to the 
north of Tompsitt Drive potentially may potentially be affected by localised 
construction noise during the noise monitoring period and/or noise associated with 
the nearby commercial/premises.  Such noise may result in higher ambient noise 
levels and subsequent project noise limits.  The residences to the south of Tompsitt 
Drive and Jerra Circle would be less exposed to such noise. 
• Access and suitability.  The noise logger was positioned to ensure local boundary 
fencing did not screen road traffic noise and localised activity from residents would 
be unlikely to affect noise levels at the microphone.  In addition, the logger was 
located on accessible land whilst minimising security risks. 
There are receptor locations nearer to the project than the monitoring location however they 
will be commercial/retail premises where the noise criteria is prescribed and not based on 
the pre-development ambient environment.  Therefore, it is not required to establish the 
ambient environment at those locations. 
The noise monitoring and subsequent data analysis was conducted in accordance with the 
NSW EPA Noise Policy for Industry (NPfI) methodology.

3.0 Assessment Criteria 
3.1 Operational Noise Criteria 
The NSW EPA Noise Policy for Industry (NPfI) sets out the requirements for the assessment 
and management of operational noise from industry in NSW. 
The NPfI defines how to determine the Project Noise Trigger Levels (PNTLs) for noise 
emissions from industrial developments.  Where a development is likely to exceed the 
PNTLs at noise-sensitive receptors, feasible and reasonable noise management measures 
are required to be considered. 
There are two types of trigger levels – one to account for ‘intrusive’ noise impacts and one to 
protect the ‘amenity’ of particular land uses: 
• The intrusiveness of an industrial noise source is generally considered acceptable if 
the LAeq noise level of the source, measured over a period of 15-minutes, does not 
exceed the representative background noise level by more than 5 dB.  Intrusive noise 
levels are only applied to residential receptors.  For other receptor types, only the 
amenity levels apply. 
• To limit continual increases in noise levels from the use of the intrusiveness level 
alone, the ambient noise level within an area from all industrial sources should 
remain below the recommended amenity levels specified in the NPfI for that 
particular land use.

3.1.2 Cumulative Noise Impacts 
The NSW Government Cumulative Impact Assessment Guidelines for State Significant 
Projects requires that the potential combined effect of cumulative impacts on all nearby 
industrial developments to be considered when assessing potential noise impacts from state 
significant projects.  The guideline references the NPfI when determining the approach to 
assessing the cumulative industrial noise impacts. 
The NPfI states that it aims to limit continuing increases in cumulative industrial noise 
through the application of amenity noise levels, which are applicable to all industrial noise 
sources in an area.   
The NPfI requires that the amenity noise levels which are applied to an individual industrial 
development be reduced by 5 dB to allow for the potential cumulative impact from multiple 
sources of industrial noise in an area (including existing and new).   
Thus, the NPfI lowers the criteria for each individual development inherently ensuring that 
the ambient noise level within an area with multiple industrial noise sources remains below 
the recommended amenity noise levels, where feasible and reasonable.  The NPfI states 
that “where the project amenity noise level applies and it can be met, no additional 
consideration of cumulative industrial noise is required”. 
The potential cumulative impacts from the development and other sources of industrial noise 
in the area are therefore accounted for in the proposal-specific PNTLs (see Table 6).

3.3 Construction Noise 
The NSW Interim Construction Noise Guideline (ICNG) is used to assess and manage 
impacts from construction noise on residences and other sensitive land uses in NSW. 
The ICNG contains procedures for determining project specific Noise Management Levels 
(NMLs) for sensitive receptors based on the existing background noise in the area.  The 
‘worst-case’ noise levels from construction of a proposal are predicted and then compared to 
the NMLs in a 15-minute assessment period to determine the likely impact of the proposal. 
The NMLs are not mandatory limits, however, where construction noise levels are predicted 
or measured to be above the NMLs, feasible and reasonable work practices to minimise 
noise emissions are to be investigated.

3.4 Vibration Criteria 
The equipment and activities associated with the data centre operations will not generate 
significant levels of vibration.  Some construction activities may be undertaken with vibration-
intensive plant items. 
The effects of vibration from construction works can be divided into three categories: 
• Those in which the occupants of buildings are disturbed (human comfort) 
• Those where building contents may be affected (building contents) 
• Those where the integrity of the building may be compromised (structural or cosmetic 
damage). 
The NSW Roads and Maritime (now Transport for NSW) Construction Noise and Vibration 
Guideline (CNVG) provides minimum working distances, outside of which impacts are not 
considered likely, for a range of common vibration-intensive construction plant items.   
The minimum working distances are based on vibration criteria provided for cosmetic 
damage (refer to British Standard (BS) 7385 Part 2 “Evaluation and measurement for 
vibration in buildings — Part 2: Guide to damage levels from groundborne vibration”) and 
human comfort (refer to the NSW “Assessing Vibration – a technical guideline”).   
They have been formulated based on empirical data which suggests that where works are 
further from receivers than the quoted minimum safe work distances, then impacts are not 
considered likely.

4.0 Prediction and Assessment Methodology 
4.1 Operational Noise Assessment 
A 3D noise model of the study area has been used to predict operational noise levels from 
the proposed development to the surrounding receptors.   
The model uses the International Organisation for Standardisation (ISO) 9613:1996 
Acoustics – Attenuation of sound during propagation outdoors – Part 2: General method of 
calculation (ISO 9613-2) prediction methodology within the SoundPLAN software package.  
ISO 9613-2 is an industry standard methodology that is considered suitable for use in the 
prediction of noise from industrial sources where intervening objects provide acoustic 
shielding, such as at the subject site and surrounding area.   
ISO 9613-2 has been used extensively on industrial projects in Australia over several 
decades and has been accepted previously by NSW DPE (now DPHI) in numerous 
environmental noise assessments. 
The noise model includes ground topography, ground absorption/reflection, buildings and 
noise sources from the development. 
The following ground absorption factors have been used (note a factor of 0.0 is fully 
reflective and 1.0 is fully absorptive): 
• Site pad – 0.0 
• Site surrounds, including built up areas – 0.25 
• Grassland beyond and to receptors – 0.75. 
The ISO 9613-2 algorithm predicts continuous A-weighted sound pressure levels under 
“noise-enhancing” meteorological conditions (ie downwind propagation from source to

The NPfI Fact Sheet C procedure for determining when to apply corrections for annoying 
noise characteristics requires at-receptor noise levels to be considered in terms of the 1/3 
octave band (“narrowband”) frequency data between 10 Hz and 10 kHz. 
Manufacturers’ noise data for mechanical plant rarely fully include the information required 
by NPfI Fact Sheet C, particularly data for frequency bands below 63 Hz.   
In lieu of suitable manufacturers’ data, NSW EPA suggest that measurement data is 
gathered at receptors near an equivalent existing facility at distances comparable to the 
receptor locations potentially affected by the proposed facility and used in the assessment of 
noise from the proposed facility1.   
That suggestion, however, requires that an equivalent existing facility exists and is 
operational, and, in the case of equivalent facilities not operated by the proponent, the facility 
and it operational details (eg plant type, numbers etc) are reasonably accessible by the 
proponent of the new proposal.

6.0 Cumulative Impacts 
The NSW Government Cumulative Impact Assessment Guidelines for State Significant 
Projects requires that the potential combined effect of cumulative impacts on all nearby 
industrial developments to be considered when assessing potential noise impacts from state 
significant projects.   
Cumulative impacts can be caused by the compounding effects of multiple projects in an 
area, and by the accumulation of effects from past, current and future activities as they arise.   
6.1 Operational Noise  
The NSW Noise Policy for Industry states that it aims to limit continuing increases in 
cumulative industrial noise through the application of amenity noise levels, which are 
applicable to all industrial noise sources in an area.   
The policy accounts for potential cumulative impacts by lowering the criteria for each 
individual development to ensure that the ambient noise level within an area from all 
industrial noise sources combined remains below the recommended amenity noise levels, 
where feasible and reasonable.   
As such (as discussed in Section 3.1.2), the potential cumulative impacts from the 
development and other potential sources of industrial noise in the area are accounted for in 
the proposal-specific PNTLs and, therefore, do not require further consideration. 
6.2 Construction Noise

near to a particular receptor at the same time.

Notwithstanding, the likelihood of worst-case noise levels at any individual receptor being 
generated by works on different projects at the same time is, however, considered low.   
The potential cumulative impacts from the proposal and other projects would continue to be 
considered as the project progresses when detailed construction planning is developed.

proposed data centre development at 300 Lanyon Drive, Jerrabomberra.  The current real 
property description of the site is Lot 6 in DP 1288362.

The potential noise and vibration emissions from the development have been assessed in 
accordance with the noise and vibration requirements stipulated in the Secretary’s 
Environmental Assessment Requirements (SEARs) issued for Project DCI CBR01 Data 
Centre (SSD-64287712), dated 6 November 2023. 
The assessment was required to establish Project Noise Trigger Levels (PNTLs) applicable 
to the development noise emissions at nearby sensitive receptors.  Noise monitoring of the 
existing ambient environment was undertaken and the results form a component in 
establishing the PNTLs for the development. 
A 3D computer model was established to predict noise from the development during 
standard operations and during regular maintenance/testing of the generators. 
The operational noise assessment has indicated that, based on the nominated mechanical 
plant and site design, specific noise controls/mitigation would not be required. 
The contractor will be responsible for ensuring the design of the generator enclosures 
(including the exhaust), include adequate mitigation/attenuation to achieve the design target 
of 85 dBA at 1 m from any part the generator enclosure including the exhaust.   
Maintenance/testing of emergency backup generators will occur four times per year.  Noise 
breakout from each generator enclosure and air intake/discharge, together with the engine 
exhaust and the associated load banks, will require judicious consideration during the 
detailed design stage.   
Noise breakout from the data centre buildings, in particular via the roof and south-facing 
facade to the mechanical corridor, would also require consideration during the detailed 
design to ensure that the building constructions are adequate to achieve the PNTLs. 
The potential operational noise emissions and subsequent requirements for mitigation would 
be confirmed during the detailed design stages when the mechanical services requirements 
and design are finalised.  This includes a detailed assessment using sound data suitable for 
the application of NPfI Fact Sheet C. 
With the inclusion of adequate mitigation, operational noise levels are expected to comply 
with the NSW NPfI PNTLs at all of the nearest identified receptors.   
Noise from road traffic related to the development, including construction and operational 
vehicles, would not be expected to exceed the NSW RMS Road Noise Policy screening 
threshold as the vehicle numbers would be very low relative to those on Environa Drive and 
Tompsitt Drive. 
Noise from construction activities and likely plant/equipment has also been considered. 
Minor exceedances of the construction Noise Management Levels are predicted at the 
nearest sensitive receptors during some of the noisier scenarios, particularly when noise-
intensive items of equipment, such as rockbreakers, are in use.   
Mitigation, noise control treatments and management practices should be implemented 
where reasonable and feasible to so.  A range of suitable measures has been provided in 
this report and would be confirmed as part of a site-specific Construction Noise and Vibration 
Management Plan (CNVMP). 
Based on the predicted noise levels and mitigation measures, noise and vibration emissions 
from the construction and operation of the development would be expected to comply with 
the project criteria, and thus would be unlikely to be considered offensive or result in adverse 
impacts at nearby sensitive receptors.
