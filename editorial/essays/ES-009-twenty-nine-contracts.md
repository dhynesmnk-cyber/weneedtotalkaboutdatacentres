## The common perception

Two pictures of Palantir in Australia reach the public. One is fear. An online video puts it in its title: "Global Surveillance, 'Pre-Crime' Arrests, & AI" [SRC_GNCA_VIDEO]. The other is reassurance. When Palantir announced in November 2025 that it had passed a government security assessment, it said its Australian platform is "hosted in Australian Amazon Web Services (AWS) regions" [SRC_PALANTIR_IRAP_NOV25].

The public record cannot settle either, because the contracts are silent where it matters. Commonwealth agencies have published 29 contracts with Palantir, and not one records where the software is hosted or where the data sits [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610] [RG-087]. Whose rules reach public data depends on where it is held, and by whom [RG-087].

## What the records show

### Building the ledger

The Commonwealth publishes every contract it signs on a website called AusTender [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. That website turns automated readers away, and so does the government's open-data portal [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. The data is free to republish, though, and the Open Contracting Partnership, an international transparency group, keeps a monthly copy [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. We read its copy of 13 October 2026 and checked it against five contracts we had already read on AusTender itself [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. Every figure matched [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610].

The ledger begins on 21 May 2013, with a software licence for Defence bought from Palantir's American parent company [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. From then to April 2026, five agencies published 29 contracts with Palantir, worth $68,258,634.93 at their latest values, not counting one that was cancelled [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]:

- the Department of Defence, 11 contracts worth $29,984,631.37 [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610];
- AUSTRAC, the agency that tracks money moving through Australia's banks, six worth $28,117,548.41 [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610];
- the Australian Criminal Intelligence Commission, five worth $6,068,875.14 [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610];
- the Australian Signals Directorate, the signals intelligence agency, six worth $4,037,580.01 [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610];
- the Department of Veterans' Affairs, one for "Specialist Data Analyst services" worth $50,000 [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610].

Defence's total bears out the reporting. In February 2026, Crikey put Defence's Palantir spending since 2013 at "more than $26 million" [SRC_CRIKEY_DEF_FEB26].

Most of Defence's money is recent. Its three contracts since October 2024, for "Data Services" in its Commercial Group and for software and an "ICT System Platform" in its Cyber Warfare Division, account for $21,674,971.89 of its $29,984,631.37 at their latest values [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610] [SRC_AUSTENDER_CN4218984A1].

> [!FIGURE] $68.3m
> The 29 contracts at their latest values, May 2013 to April 2026 [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]

The surprise was the second-biggest customer. AUSTRAC has bought Palantir's software since 2016, and its "Data Analytics capability" contract of January 2023, worth $12,074,872.31, is the largest single contract in the ledger [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. In 2026 it added a "Transaction Reporting and Integration Platform" and the services to implement a "Transaction Reporting solution" [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. The agency that watches the banks is one of Palantir's two biggest Commonwealth customers [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610].

### How they were bought

Nineteen of the 29 were limited tenders, worth $26,124,625.09 in all [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. In a limited tender the government approaches one supplier, or a few, instead of the open market [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. All six of the Signals Directorate's contracts were limited tenders, and so were nine of Defence's eleven [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. AUSTRAC's six went to open or prequalified tenders [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610].

Eleven name the American parent, Palantir Technologies Inc., rather than its Australian subsidiary, and together they are worth $10,009,817.59 [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. Five of the Signals Directorate's six do [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610].

What the agencies bought, the titles describe only in kind: "Software Licencing", "Software Support", "Data Services", "Data Analytics capability" [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. They are licences, support, data services and analytics, and not one says what the software is used for [RG-090] [RG-093]. Defence's newest is an "ICT System Platform" for its Cyber Warfare Division, a limited tender of $7,639,894.90 published in February 2026 and amended in April to $10,389,894.90 [SRC_AUSTENDER_CN4218984] [SRC_AUSTENDER_CN4218984A1].

### The promise and the register

Palantir's promise is about its platform. Its announcement of 20 November 2025 says it passed an assessment at the PROTECTED level under the Information Security Registered Assessors Program, carried out by an independent assessor, and that its Australian platform is "hosted in Australian Amazon Web Services (AWS) regions" [SRC_PALANTIR_IRAP_NOV25].

The government keeps its own register of certified hosting, the Hosting Certification Framework [SRC_HCF_REGISTER]. Among its certified strategic cloud services is "Amazon Web Services - Cloud Service offering (Australian Regions)" [SRC_HCF_REGISTER]. Palantir is not on it [SRC_HCF_REGISTER].

Should it be? Not necessarily. The register certifies hosting, not software, and Palantir says it runs on regions the register already certifies [RG-089] [SRC_PALANTIR_IRAP_NOV25]. It could not have joined lately in any case. The Department paused new registrations from 3 November 2025, seventeen days before Palantir's announcement, "until HCF reforms have been completed" [SRC_HCF_REGISTER].

So the promise may well be true of the platform. The gap is between the platform and the contracts [RG-087]. No notice says whether an agency's system runs on that platform, on the agency's own premises, or anywhere else [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. This record does not tell you where any agency's Palantir system runs. The hosting terms of each contract would [RG-087].

### The agency the public cannot ask

Australia's freedom of information law lists the agencies it does not reach, and the Australian Signals Directorate is one [SRC_FOI_ACT_1982]. Any document that came from it is out of reach too, whichever agency holds it [SRC_FOI_ACT_1982]. Defence is exempt only for documents on operational intelligence and special access programs; the criminal intelligence commission and AUSTRAC are not exempt agencies, although AUSTRAC's intelligence documents are [SRC_FOI_ACT_1982].

So the six contracts the Signals Directorate holds, five of them with the American parent, sit outside the public's right to ask [SRC_FOI_ACT_1982] [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610].

The same wall stands in front of a puzzle. In February 2026, Crikey reported: "In November 2024, the Australian Signals Directorate granted the firm a top security clearance." [SRC_CRIKEY_DEF_FEB26] Palantir's own announcement of its PROTECTED assessment is dated a year later [SRC_PALANTIR_IRAP_NOV25]. Two dates, for what may be two different things, and the agency at the centre cannot be asked [RG-089]. Defence can be asked about its own records, and that application is drafted [RG-089].

### Where the fear came from

The video's description credits its framing to NVIDIA's chief executive, saying NVIDIA would "accelerate everything that Palantir does" [SRC_GNCA_VIDEO]. Two independent transcripts of his keynote in Washington on 28 October 2025 give the longer sentence: "We work with Palantir to accelerate everything Palantir does", processing data "for our government, for national security, and for enterprises around the world" [SRC_SHERWOOD_GTC_QUOTE] [SRC_SINGJU_GTC_TRANSCRIPT]. Our read: speaking in Washington, he meant the American government [SRC_SHERWOOD_GTC_QUOTE]. The recording itself turned our crawler away, so the line rests on the two transcripts until a person checks it [RG-092]. No Commonwealth contract notice names NVIDIA as a supplier [RG-090].

### The picture that belongs to someone else

In July 2026, WA Police began trialling live facial recognition from a van in Perth [SRC_GUARDIAN_WA_FRT]. In its first week it scanned more than 130,000 faces and led to 19 arrests [SRC_GUARDIAN_WA_FRT]. The Guardian's report of the launch did not name the supplier [SRC_GUARDIAN_WA_FRT].

WA Police's own privacy assessment does. It names NEC and its NeoFace Watch system, states "Data stored in Australia", and does not mention Palantir [SRC_WAPOL_OLFR_PIA]. A state police privacy assessment told the public where its data is kept. None of the 29 Commonwealth contracts with Palantir does [SRC_WAPOL_OLFR_PIA] [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610].

Palantir's place in Australian law enforcement is quieter, and older. The criminal intelligence commission has bought its software since 2014, and AUSTRAC since 2016 [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. What either runs on it is not stated anywhere we have found [RG-093].

### The supermarket that said where

Coles signed with Palantir in February 2024 for rostering, store operations and supply chain planning across more than 840 stores [SRC_ITNEWS_COLES_SEP26]. After a campaign that gathered 86,000 petition signatures, a Coles spokesperson said on 4 September 2026: "Coles and Palantir have agreed to conclude their partnership after three years of collaboration." [SRC_ITNEWS_COLES_SEP26] Coles gave no reason, and its notice for the November annual meeting lists five items of business, none about Palantir [RG-095] [SRC_COLES_AGM_NOM_2026].

Coles did say where the data was: "The software is deployed within Coles' own environment and is operated and isolated under Coles controls. Palantir does not control the data, nor can it independently repurpose it." [SRC_ITNEWS_COLES_SEP26] A supermarket has told the public more about where its Palantir data sits than any Commonwealth contract has [SRC_ITNEWS_COLES_SEP26] [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610].

### Where government data may sit

The government describes its top hosting certification as representing "the highest level of assurance to Australian Government customers" and "the most secure storage solutions for government held data" [SRC_HCF_REGISTER]. Its list gives each certified facility's operator and name, and nothing else: no state, no street [RG-101]. Reading it end to end, we found thirteen certified facilities our own records lacked [RG-101].

One was DigiCo's SYD1 in Sydney, which its annual report says holds "Strategic Certification" under the framework [SRC_DGT_AR_2026]. In October 2026, DigiCo announced a 15-year contract for 71 megawatts there with "a global technology firm", which it did not name [SRC_DGT_SYD1_71MW_20261015]. The government's list of where its most sensitive data may sit does not say where those places are, and one of them is leased to a tenant the public cannot name [RG-101].

### How the pieces fit

For any one contract, the trail runs like this [RG-087]:

1. The contract notice names the agency, the supplier and the value [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610].
2. The supplier's announcement says where its Australian platform runs [SRC_PALANTIR_IRAP_NOV25].
3. The certification register lists the cloud regions certified for government data [SRC_HCF_REGISTER].
4. The contract's hosting terms would join the three. They are not published, and for the Signals Directorate they cannot be asked for [SRC_FOI_ACT_1982] [RG-087].

## Where the data is too thin

- Where each Defence deployment runs, and whether its data can be reached from outside Australia. An application to Defence is drafted [RG-087].
- What, if anything, was granted in November 2024, and by whom [RG-089].
- What the criminal intelligence commission runs on Palantir's software. An application to it is drafted [RG-093].
- What any state or territory has bought. Victoria, South Australia, the ACT and Western Australia turned our crawler away, as New South Wales had before; a person can search them [RG-086].
- Whether Palantir holds any AUKUS work. No contract notice says so [RG-090].
- Whether Westpac uses Palantir. The claims rest on a report of an executive's social media post and a campaign group's statement [RG-088].
- Which firm holds DigiCo's Sydney contract [RG-101].

## What to watch next

Two applications to Defence and one to the criminal intelligence commission are drafted [RG-087] [RG-089] [RG-093]. Under the Commonwealth law an agency must decide within 30 days, with 30 more if it consults a third party such as Palantir [RG-087]. Replies will become a follow-up piece, not an edit to this one.

Coles' arrangement is not expected to run beyond 2027 [SRC_ITNEWS_COLES_SEP26]. And the hosting register is being reformed; what the reformed register certifies, and whether it says where, is worth watching [SRC_HCF_REGISTER].

### For journalists and researchers

The contract feed is free to republish, and the Open Contracting Partnership's copy can be filtered by supplier to rebuild every total here [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. Use the latest value of each notice, as we did, and expect the feed's counts to differ slightly from the website's, as its publisher warns [SRC_AUSTENDER_OCDS_202610]. The application to Defence asks only where the software and data sit, not what the software does, framed with the operational-intelligence exemption in mind [RG-087]. Any reader can lodge the same request.

## Our view

Where public data sits should be a term of the public contract, and published with it.

Today the public has three things: the supplier's word about its platform, a register that certifies the cloud underneath, and silence in the contracts that would join them. All of it may be in order. Nobody outside the agencies can tell.

The fear in the video's title is not what the record shows. In Western Australia, at least, the facial recognition belongs to another company. The reassurance is not shown either, because it is a promise about a platform, not about any agency's system.

The fix costs a line on a form. Every Commonwealth notice for software that handles government data should state where the data is held and under which certification. An agency outside the freedom of information law can still publish that much about its own contracts.

> To the Secretary, Department of Defence: for each of the Department's current contracts with Palantir Technologies Australia Pty Ltd published on AusTender, in which country and which facility is Commonwealth data stored or processed, and under which certification? Is data residency a term of each contract?
