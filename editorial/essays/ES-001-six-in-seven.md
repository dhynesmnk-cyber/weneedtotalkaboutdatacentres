## The common perception

In August 2026 the NSW Government published its rules for data centres [SRC_NSWGUIDE26]. They tell the public that "Oxford Economics, which prepared AEMO's forecast on data centres, estimates 6 in every 7 megawatts (MW) of data centre connection requests are phantom demand." [SRC_NSWGUIDE26]

AEMO is the Australian Energy Market Operator, which runs the national electricity market [SRC_NSWGUIDE26]. The firm's name and that connection carry the weight of the sentence: this is the forecaster the market itself relies on [SRC_NSWGUIDE26].

It is a calming number. It says most of the power the industry asks for will never be drawn, so most of the alarm about data centres and the grid is about megawatts that do not exist [SRC_OXEC_PHANTOM_2025].

> [!FIGURE] 6 in 7
> Megawatts of data centre connection requests that the NSW rules describe as phantom demand [SRC_NSWGUIDE26]

## What the records show

The number comes from a research briefing that Oxford Economics Australia published on 21 November 2025, under the subtitle "Surging data centre connection requests drive phantom demand for power" [SRC_OXEC_PHANTOM_2025]. It counts 44 gigawatts of connection requests made to the market operator and finds that six in every seven megawatts of them are phantom [SRC_OXEC_PHANTOM_2025].

The rules are right that the firm works for the market operator. The briefing's own page says AEMO separately commissioned Oxford Economics to forecast how much electricity data centres will use [SRC_OXEC_PHANTOM_2025]. But the paper the rules cite is not that forecast. It is the briefing, and its page ends: "This research briefing was commissioned by AWS. The analysis and findings are Oxford Economics." [SRC_OXEC_PHANTOM_2025]

> [!PULLQUOTE]
> "This research briefing was commissioned by AWS." [SRC_OXEC_PHANTOM_2025]

AWS is one of the companies the Business Council of Australia lists as building and operating data centres across the country [SRC_BCA_SUB116]. The NSW rules cite the briefing by title in a footnote and introduce its author as the firm that prepared the market operator's forecast [SRC_NSWGUIDE26]. They do not mention AWS [SRC_NSWGUIDE26].

The number reached the rulebook by way of the NSW parliamentary inquiry into data centres. The Business Council's submission, received on 2 April 2026, cites the briefing and puts the requests at around 44 GW, the capacity expected to proceed at about 6 GW, and the electricity actually drawn at maturity at 2.8 GW, "15 times lower than what is sometimes reported" [SRC_BCA_SUB116]. At the hearing on 22 May, the committee's Chair observed that Data Centres Australia and the Business Council had both cited the phantom demand research [SRC_INQ_T_0522].

Who speaks through the Business Council? Its member list runs to 130 organisations [SRC_BCA_MEMBERS_202610]. Among them are AirTrunk and Blackstone, whose purchase of AirTrunk closed in December 2024 at A$24 billion [SRC_BCA_MEMBERS_202610] [SRC_AIRTRUNK_BC]. So are Amazon, Google, Microsoft and Meta, the developers Goodman and Stockland, and two of the network businesses that connect data centres to the grid, TransGrid and Endeavour Energy [SRC_BCA_MEMBERS_202610]. Both networks gave evidence to the same inquiry [SRC_INQ_T_0522] [SRC_INQ_T_0529].

The meetings that shaped the rules happened, on the government's own account. The Secretary of the Premier's Department told the inquiry they had "met with Data Centres Australia and talked to them about the consultation paper" [SRC_INQ_T_0522]. The Chief Executive of Infrastructure NSW told it that "a lot of those organisations have made direct representations to different people in government", and that "There is no Minister for data centres." [SRC_INQ_T_0522]

## Where the data is too thin

Nobody has yet said when those meetings took place, or who else was in them [RG-070]. NSW ministers publish their diaries every quarter, and the index of the 2026 disclosures is public [SRC_NSW_DIARIES_2026]. The diaries themselves sit in a folder that the government's website asks automated readers to stay out of, so the thirty that matter, for the Premier, the Treasurer and the planning, energy and industry ministers from January 2025 to June 2026, have not yet been read [SRC_NSW_DIARIES_2026] [RG-070]. The state's lobbyist register turns automated readers away entirely, and the Commonwealth's will only open in a person's browser [RG-070].

What AWS paid for the briefing, and on what terms, is not stated anywhere this observatory has found [RG-078].

Whether the Business Council's positions on data centres come from its members or from its board is not something its public records say [RG-067].

## What to watch next

The diaries and the lobbyist registers can be downloaded by a person, and they are the next documents this observatory will read [RG-070].

The same NSW rules carry a demand-flexibility requirement and design limits on how efficiently data centres use energy and water [SRC_NSWGUIDE26]. How those limits are written into each site's conditions will show how much the phantom demand framing shaped what was actually required [SRC_NSWGUIDE26].

## Our view

A rulebook that leans on a number should say who paid for it.

This is not a claim that the number is wrong. Oxford Economics says the analysis and findings are its own, and nothing in the record says otherwise. Phantom demand may be real, and it may be large. The point is narrower and more important: the NSW rules introduced the figure with the firm's public credentials and left out its commercial ones. A reader of the rules has no way of knowing that the most reassuring sentence in them traces back to a briefing commissioned by a company that builds and runs data centres here.

That is how a narrative is built without anyone telling a lie. A company commissions research. Its peak body cites the research to a parliamentary inquiry. The inquiry hears it from several directions at once, so it sounds like consensus. By the time it reaches the government's rulebook, the commissioning has dropped away and only the credentials remain.

The fix costs nothing. Any figure in government guidance that comes from commissioned research should name who commissioned it, in the text, not in a footnote. And a diary that is public only to people with the time to download thirty files one by one is not fully public. Transparency that works only by attrition favours the people who are paid to be patient.

Data centres will be built here, and some should be. The question is who gets to set the terms of the conversation about how many, how big, and on whose power. Right now, the answer includes the people selling the answer.
