## What it rests on

The scale of what is proposed is very large. By the Business Council of Australia's account, the market operator received around 44 gigawatts of connection requests linked to data centre proposals in 2025 [SRC_BCA_SUB116].

So is the money. The largest data centre deal on record, the sale of AirTrunk to Blackstone and CPP Investments, closed at A$24 billion in December 2024 [SRC_AIRTRUNK_BC]. Some of the money is already ours: at the end of June 2026, Cbus's Growth option alone held A$344.5 million of shares in NEXTDC [SRC_CBUS_PHD_202606].

The rules are being written now, and not always on neutral evidence. The most reassuring figure in the debate, that six in every seven megawatts of connection requests are phantom demand, came from a briefing commissioned by AWS [SRC_OXEC_PHANTOM_2025]. It reached the NSW Government's rules without that being said [SRC_NSWGUIDE26].

One frontier AI lab is already lining up as a tenant. In September 2026 Dexus told the stock exchange that the consortium behind a proposed data centre in Queensland's Western Downs had entered into lease documentation with Anthropic's Australian subsidiary, subject to customary approvals [SRC_DEXUS_WDDP_2026].

Some of the right instruments already exist. The NSW rules set design limits on how efficiently a data centre uses energy and water, and require some of its demand to be flexible [SRC_NSWGUIDE26].

## Our view

Data centres are needed. Part of the problem is that we are not dreaming loudly enough about what they could be.

Hyperscale data centres, as they are being built now, are not necessary for human flourishing. They are a nightmare for the communities that live beside them and for the land and water they draw on. But the shape of this industry does not have to be set by the people with the biggest bank transfers.

This observatory keeps the record and the argument apart, so that the argument can be checked against the record. This page is the argument, plainly. The essays test it; the record is there for anyone who wants to test it themselves.

What if Australia set the terms? The ideas we are working on, each to be argued in full, with the rules here and the precedents overseas, in the "What if…?" pages:

- **Scale with a reason.** Anything above a standard size needs a market justification study, with only narrow national security waivers.
- **Standards that ratchet.** Energy and water efficiency mandated at the best the technology achieves, and carbon-free supply pegged to the renewable potential of the place, reviewed every year and only ever tightened. Zoning that requires water to be cleaned and returned, or cooling that does not evaporate it.
- **Less dark data.** Requirements to minimise, review and delete data that is stored and never used, with fines that recur for as long as the breach does.
- **Bring your own power.** A grid connection only on proof that the facility's energy use is net neutral, or that it is funding the generation to make it so.
- **Governance that includes the people who live there.** First Nations and community governance requirements, with a share of the computing capacity built held for local and sovereign projects.

We think Australia has what a pro-human data centre industry needs: land, sun, wind, and a stable democracy. We think the companies that want to build here need that more than we need any one of them. That is a strong position from which to ask for more.
